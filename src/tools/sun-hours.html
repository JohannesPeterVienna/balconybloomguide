This simulator estimates how many hours of direct sun three spots on your balcony get on a clear day in each month: the top of the railing, the middle of the floor and the back wall. The numbers often differ by several hours on the same balcony, which is why a plant label that says "full sun" is only half the answer.

How to use it

You need your location, the compass direction your balcony faces looking out from the back wall, and a few measurements: depth from the wall to the railing, width along the facade, height of the ceiling (the slab or balcony above), or no ceiling if you have open sky. Note whether there are full-height side walls on the left or right, how tall the railing is and whether it is solid, glass or open bars. If another building stands across the street or courtyard, add its distance from your railing and how far its roof rises above your floor.

A phone compass works for the direction; hold it flat and away from the metal railing. For the opposite building, count its floors above yours and allow about 10 ft (3 m) per floor.

How it calculates

For the 15th of every month in 2026, the simulator works out the sun's height and compass bearing every 2 minutes through the day using the NOAA solar position equations. Sun lower than 2° above the horizon is not counted.

The balcony is treated as a box. From each of the three spots, the simulator follows a straight line toward the sun and checks whether it is blocked. A spot gets no sun when the sun is behind the facade, when the line leaves the box through a side that has a wall, when it hits the ceiling slab, when it passes below the top of a solid railing, or when it passes below the top of the building opposite. That building is modeled as a long wall parallel to yours. Glass and open railings let the sun through.

The three spots sit on the center line of the balcony. The railing spot is at the height of the railing top, 6 in (15 cm) in from its outer line – where the plants in a box hung on the inside of the railing grow. The middle spot is halfway between wall and railing, 1 ft (30 cm) above the floor, roughly the top of a medium pot. The back-wall spot is 1 ft (30 cm) out from the wall at the same plant height. Each month is classed as full sun (6 hours or more), part sun (3 to 6 hours) or shade (under 3 hours). Times of day are local solar time, where noon is the moment the sun is highest; your clock can differ from that by an hour or more, mostly because of daylight saving time and where you sit within your time zone.

Worked example: an east-facing balcony in New York

Take a balcony in New York City (latitude 40.7° N) facing due east: 5 ft (1.5 m) deep, 10 ft (3 m) wide, ceiling at 8.5 ft (2.6 m), side walls on both sides and a 3.5 ft (1.07 m) open railing. On June 15 the railing gets 6.9 hours of direct sun, from about 4:46 to 11:38 solar time. The back wall gets sun from the same moment but loses it at about 10:02, when the rising sun disappears behind the ceiling slab: 5.3 hours in total.

Turn the same balcony to face south and the pattern flips with the season. In December all three spots get 7.7 to 8.7 hours, but in June only the railing gets sun (6.9 hours): the midsummer sun stands too high to shine under an 8.5 ft ceiling onto the floor. See south-facing balcony plants for what that means. In the Southern Hemisphere north and south swap roles; the simulator handles that through your latitude.

Limits

  • Clear sky only. Clouds, haze and smog are not included, so real totals are lower in cloudy months.
  • Trees, balconies beside you, awnings and plants are not modeled. A building opposite is one long wall of even height.
  • Glass railings count as fully clear. Frosted or tinted glass lets through less light; if yours is frosted, also run it as solid and treat the truth as somewhere between.

FAQ

Why does the railing get more sun than the back wall?

Every obstacle cuts off a larger slice of sky the further back you stand. The railing sees almost the whole sky in front of the facade; the back wall sees a narrow window.

Does sun before 9 a.m. count as full sun?

Yes, in hours it counts the same. In practice morning sun is cooler than afternoon sun, so 6 morning hours stress plants less than 6 afternoon hours, and heat-loving crops may ripen more slowly.

Why do the times not match my clock?

They are local solar time, with noon when the sun is highest. Add an hour during daylight saving time, then a further shift depending on where you are in your time zone.

What do I do with the hours?

Pick plants by the class of the spot where the pot will stand, and use the hours in the watering planner, since sunnier pots dry faster.
