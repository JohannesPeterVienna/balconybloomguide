When can the tomato pot go outside, and is the season long enough for it? This planner starts from two dates – the last spring freeze and the first fall freeze – and counts from them with the planting rules of a university extension service. For 53 U.S. cities the dates come from NOAA's 1991–2020 climate normals; anywhere else you enter your own.

How to use it

Pick the station nearest to you, or choose "Other place" and enter the frost dates your national weather service or local gardening organization publishes. Choose whether to plan with the median date or the cautious one. The result lists, for each crop, when to sow indoors (where the source gives a transplant age; "–" otherwise), the earliest date to plant or sow outdoors, a first harvest estimate and whether the frost-free period is long enough. The last two columns give the smallest container a university source names for the crop and what the wet mix in it weighs; the Pot Weight Planner adds the pot itself.

Use "Copy link" to keep or share a plan, "Download CSV" for a spreadsheet and "Print plan" for paper.

How it calculates

Frost dates. NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information publish, for each station, the dates by which the last 32 °F (0 °C) reading of spring and the first of fall occur at fixed probability levels. The planner stores three levels per season. "Median" uses the 50 % dates. "Cautious" uses the spring date that only one year in ten exceeds and the fall date that only one year in ten undercuts.

Planting out. Colorado State University Extension sorts vegetables into four frost classes and ties each to the average last spring frost: hardy crops as early as 2–4 weeks before it, semi-hardy crops 0–2 weeks before it, tender crops from seed around that date, very tender crops typically 2 or more weeks after it. The planner adds those offsets to the date you chose.

Sowing indoors. For crops usually set out as transplants, the same source gives the age of a transplant in weeks. The planner subtracts that age from the planting-out window. Cucumbers and squash are best sown direct; the indoor date is shown only because the source allows transplants no older than 2–3 weeks.

Season check. The days from the end of the planting-out window to the first fall freeze are compared with the typical days to harvest. "Fits" means at least four weeks to spare, "tight" less than that, "too short" fewer days than the crop typically needs. The four-week margin is this site's own caution mark; days to harvest vary by variety and slow down in cool weather. For very tender crops the check is only a count of days: the Colorado guide wants summer-like weather with days consistently above 55 °F (13 °C) and warns that a week below that may stunt the crop, so each of those rows carries the condition, and "long enough by the calendar" in a cool-summer place such as Anchorage does not mean the crop will ripen.

Container and weight. University of Maryland Extension groups container crops by the volume of growing media they need. The planner converts gallons to litres and multiplies by the wet weight per litre used throughout this site: 0.75 kg for standard potting mix, 0.6 kg for a lightweight mix.

How to read the probability dates

A frost date is not a promise. In the Central Park record the median last spring freeze is March 30, but in one year out of ten a freeze still comes after April 9. Planning with the median means accepting an even chance of one more frost after your date; tender plants in pots can be carried indoors for a night, which is the balcony gardener's advantage over a garden bed.

NOAA's variable list describes the spring dates as the "probability date of last 32F occurrence or earlier". In the data the 10 % date is the latest of the set and the 90 % date the earliest, so we read them in the direction that fits the order of the dates: a 10 % chance that the last freeze comes later than the 10 % date. The fall dates read as written: a 10 % chance that the first freeze comes on or before the 10 % date.

The stations are airports and city observatories. A balcony against a heated wall, under a slab and out of the wind usually runs milder than open ground; a top-floor corner does not. Five stations in the list – Honolulu, Los Angeles, Miami, San Diego and San Francisco – have no freeze dates because 32 °F did not occur in the 30 years. At four more a freeze came in fewer than nine years out of ten – Phoenix (17.5 %), Tampa (34.5 %), Orlando (60.7 %) and New Orleans (86.8 %) – and the median first and last freeze both fall in mid-winter. For these nine stations the planner shows what NOAA reports and gives no schedule, because counting from frost dates would mislead there. The nine-in-ten line is this site's own cut-off.

Limits

  • The dates are the earliest by frost alone. The Colorado State guide also ties planting to soil temperature, and for very tender crops to settled weather with days above 55 °F (13 °C).
  • Its figures were written for open ground in Colorado. The frost classes carry over to other temperate climates; the days to harvest are typical values, not a forecast for a pot on your balcony.
  • Outside the United States the planner has no station data. The arithmetic works with any pair of frost dates, including Southern Hemisphere dates, because it counts forward through the calendar.
  • Where winters are frost-free, heat and day length decide the planting calendar, not frost. Use your regional extension calendar.
  • Light is a separate question: a crop planted on time still fails in the wrong spot. Check the hours with the Sun & Shade Simulator.

FAQ

Is 32 °F the right threshold for my plants?

It is the freezing point and the threshold the planting rules refer to. Very tender crops such as peppers and eggplant suffer well above it, which is why the rule keeps them back two weeks or more.

Why does my city show a date that seems early?

City-centre and airport stations in mild locations freeze late in fall and early in spring, and the median is exceeded every second year. Switch to the cautious dates and compare with your local extension calendar.

Can I use it for flowers?

Only indirectly. The frost classes come from a vegetable guide. For tender bedding plants the last-frost date itself is the useful number.

Does a pot change the dates?

A pot warms faster than garden soil in spring and cools faster in fall, and it can be moved. The planner does not model that; it gives the open-ground rule for your dates.
