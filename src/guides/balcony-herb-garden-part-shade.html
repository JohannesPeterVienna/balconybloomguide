A balcony with 3 to 6 hours of direct sun is a good place for leafy herbs: parsley, chives, mint, cilantro, chervil, lemon balm and sorrel all do well there, and several of them actually last longer than they would in full sun. The woody Mediterranean herbs (rosemary, thyme, oregano, sage) and warmth-loving basil are the ones that struggle, and they belong only on the sunniest spot you have, usually right at the railing.

Leafy herbs versus Mediterranean herbs

The split is not random. Herbs grown for soft leaves come from cooler, moister places or are fast annuals that bolt (run to flower and seed) when they get hot and dry. Shade slows that down. Herbs grown for aromatic oils come from dry, sunny hillsides; with less sun they grow long and floppy, taste milder and sit in damp potting mix longer, which is the usual cause of root rot in a pot.

Basil (Ocimum basilicum) is a special case. It is less about sun hours and more about warmth: it stalls in cool nights and blackens with cold. A sheltered, warm corner with around 5 to 6 hours of sun often grows better basil than an exposed spot with more sun and a cold wind. Wait until nights stay above about 50°F (10°C) before putting it out.

Railing or back wall: where the sun actually lands

On most balconies the sun hours change a lot within a few feet. The balcony above acts as a roof: in summer, when the sun is high around midday, its edge keeps the back half of the floor in shade, so the back wall often gets sun only in the morning or late afternoon, when the sun is low enough to reach under the ceiling. A solid railing does the opposite and shades pots standing on the floor right behind it.

That gives a simple placement rule. The sun-hungriest pot goes on a railing planter or a stand at railing height, where it clears a solid railing and still sits outside the shadow of the ceiling. The most shade-tolerant herbs go at the back wall. The middle of the floor is in between. A three-pot plan for a part-shade balcony

Three containers are enough for a steady supply of kitchen herbs, and keeping them separate lets each one sit where it suits best. Measure your sun hours in June or July first; the plan below assumes roughly 5 to 6 hours at the railing and 2 to 4 at the back wall.

  1. Pot 1, railing (sunniest): a railing planter or trough about 24 in (60 cm) long and 8 in (20 cm) deep with two parsley plants and one clump of chives. If your railing gets a full 6 hours or more, swap one parsley for thyme or basil. See railing planter sizes and brackets before hanging anything over the edge.
  2. Pot 2, middle of the floor: mint alone in a 12 in (30 cm) pot. Mint spreads by runners and will swamp anything sharing its pot within a season. Stand the pot on feet or a tray with a lip so runners cannot escape through the drainage hole into a neighboring container.
  3. Pot 3, back wall (least sun): a trough about 20 in (50 cm) long and 8 in deep for chervil and cilantro, sown in short rows every three to four weeks from spring to early fall. Both bolt quickly in heat, and the back wall is the coolest spot you have. In midsummer, when they bolt fastest, sorrel or lemon balm can take over this pot.

Weight adds up faster than it looks: three pots of this size hold several gallons of wet mix. The Pot Weight Planner gives you the total if you need to show it to your building manager.

Part-shade pots dry more slowly than sunny ones. Water the railing pot more often than the back-wall trough, and check each with a finger rather than watering everything on the same schedule.

How your balcony's direction changes the plan

East-facing balconies get cool morning sun and are close to ideal for leafy herbs; the east-facing guide covers what else fits there. West-facing balconies get hot afternoon sun, which suits rosemary at the railing but makes cilantro bolt, so keep cilantro well back. North-facing balconies often get only early and late sun in summer; chervil, mint and parsley are the realistic choices. These directions apply in the Northern Hemisphere; south of the equator, swap north and south.

Floor level matters too. On high, windy floors leaves dry out and tear, and soft herbs suffer more than woody ones. A railing pot on an exposed corner may need the shelter of the middle floor even though it gets less sun there.

Harvesting, winter and pets

Cut leafy herbs often and from the outside of the plant; regular cutting keeps parsley and chives producing and delays flowering. Pinch out basil flower spikes as they appear. Chives, mint, sorrel and lemon balm are perennials that die back or slow down in winter and come back in spring; parsley is a biennial that usually flowers and finishes in its second year. For how containers cope with frost, see overwintering balcony plants.

If you have a cat or dog, note that chives belong to the onion family (Allium), which is toxic to both. Several other common herbs, including mint, oregano, parsley and lemon balm, also appear on the ASPCA toxic plant list. Keep those out of reach of a pet that chews leaves.

FAQ

Can I grow rosemary on a balcony with only 4 hours of sun?

It will usually survive the summer but grow sparse, with weak flavor, and it is more likely to rot because the mix stays wet. Give it the sunniest railing spot, a gritty, free-draining mix, and water only when the top inch (2.5 cm) is dry.

Why does my cilantro keep flowering so fast?

Heat, long days and dry soil all trigger bolting. Move it to the coolest, shadiest spot, keep the mix evenly moist and sow a new batch every few weeks instead of expecting one sowing to last.

Can I plant mint with other herbs to save space?

Not for long. Mint's runners fill the pot and crowd out its neighbors within one season. Give it its own container.

How do I know how many hours of sun each spot gets?

Check each spot every hour on a clear day around midsummer, or use the Sun & Shade Simulator, which estimates railing, middle and back wall separately by month.
