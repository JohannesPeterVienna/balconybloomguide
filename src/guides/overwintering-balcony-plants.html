A plant that is perfectly hardy in a garden bed can die in a pot on the same balcony, because its roots sit in a small block of soil exposed to cold air on every side. For containers that stay out all winter, a common rule is to choose plants rated about two hardiness zones colder than your own. Tender plants such as geraniums, fuchsias and citrus come indoors before the first frost.

Why roots in containers are less hardy

Hardiness ratings describe how much cold the top of a plant tolerates when it grows in the ground. Roots are usually much less cold-tolerant than stems and buds. In a garden, they are protected by the mass of soil around them, which cools slowly and rarely freezes deeply. In a pot, cold reaches the roots from the sides, the top and often from below through a cold concrete floor. A container can freeze solid during a cold spell that would barely affect the soil in a bed.

That is where the two-zone rule comes from. Find your USDA zone on the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map (each zone covers a 10 °F, about 5.6 °C, band of average annual minimum temperature), then pick plants rated at least two zones lower.

Worked example. You live in zone 7, where the average coldest winter night falls between 0 and 10 °F (−18 to −12 °C). A shrub rated hardy to zone 7 is a gamble in a pot on your balcony. One rated to zone 5 (average minimum −20 to −10 °F, −29 to −23 °C) has a real safety margin. In the UK, the RHS uses H-ratings instead; for containers left outside, a plant rated H5 or above (hardy in a cold winter) is the safer choice, and H4 plants generally need some protection.

The rule is a guide, not a guarantee. A large pot holds more soil and cools more slowly than a small one, and your balcony's microclimate can be several degrees warmer or colder than the zone map suggests.

Use your balcony's warm and cold spots

Most balconies have a clear warm side and a cold side in winter.

  • Against the building wall is usually the warmest spot. Heat from the heated rooms behind the wall and shelter from the balcony above keep it a little milder.
  • At the railing pots are exposed to wind and to open sky, which lets heat radiate away on clear nights. Glass or open-bar railings give little shelter.
  • Higher floors are usually windier, and winter wind dries out evergreen leaves quickly. Use the Wind Exposure Check to see how exposed your balcony is, and read windy balcony plants for pots that stay put.

In late fall, move every container that stays out from the railing to the back wall and group them closely. Pots in a tight group protect each other's sides from cold air.

Insulate, raise and choose the right pot

Insulate the sides. Wrap pots in bubble wrap, burlap or a layer of horticultural fleece, tied on firmly so wind cannot tear it off. Leave the top open so the plant gets air and light.

Raise them. Pot feet or small blocks lift the container off cold concrete and keep the drainage hole clear. A pot that sits in meltwater or a frozen saucer can have its roots killed by waterlogging rather than cold. Remove saucers for the winter.

Use frost-proof pots. Wet potting mix expands when it freezes, and porous pots can crack or flake. Plastic, fiberglass, wood and thick-walled stoneware labeled frost-proof usually survive. Ordinary unglazed terracotta is the most likely to crack. Pots that are narrower at the top than in the middle are risky, because the frozen root ball cannot push upward.

On an open balcony, snow adds weight on top of already wet pots. Brush heavy snow off planters and the floor rather than letting it pile up, and do not stack extra containers at the railing. The Pot Weight Planner shows what your containers weigh when wet.

Watering in winter

Plants in pots still need some water over winter, and on a covered balcony neither rain nor snow reaches them. Evergreens are the most at risk: their leaves keep losing water on sunny and windy days, and if the root ball is dry or frozen, the leaves brown from the tips.

Check the pots about every two to three weeks. Water evergreens on a mild, dry day when the mix is not frozen, in the late morning so the water can soak in before night. Give enough to moisten the root ball, not a soaking. Deciduous plants without leaves need much less; just make sure the mix does not dry out completely.

Bringing tender plants inside

Bring tender plants in before the first frost is forecast, not after. Check them for aphids, whitefly and spider mites first; pests multiply quickly in warm indoor air. A cool, bright spot suits most of them better than a warm living room, where they grow weak and leggy. Water much less than in summer. Move them back out after the last spring frost, starting in a sheltered spot for a week so they adjust to sun and wind. The balcony calendar shows when each step usually falls.

FAQ

Can I leave pots outside in winter if the plant is hardy?

Often yes, if the plant is rated about two zones colder than yours, the pot is frost-proof and reasonably large, and you place it against the wall. Small pots are the riskiest.

Should I cover plants with plastic?

Not tightly over the leaves. Plastic traps moisture and heats up in sun. Use breathable fleece over the plant and keep plastic or bubble wrap for the pot sides.

My evergreen turned brown in February. Is it dead?

Not necessarily. Browning after sunny, windy spells is often drying, not frost. Wait until late spring before pruning; scratch a stem with a fingernail, and green tissue underneath means it is alive.
