Nobody can tell from the outside how much a particular balcony can hold. What you can do is work out what your planters weigh when they are wet, put the heaviest ones where the structure is strongest, and give your building manager real numbers to check. One large pot of wet potting mix can weigh as much as an adult.

What a planter really weighs

The weight that matters is the wet weight, right after watering or a long rain. Water alone weighs about 8.3 lb per gallon (1 kg per liter), and a pot full of mix holds a lot of it. Most bagged peat- or coir-based potting mixes weigh roughly 5 to 7.5 lb per gallon (0.6 to 0.9 kg per liter) once watered and drained. Garden soil and loam-based mixes are much heavier; the lightweight potting mix guide compares them.

The container adds its share. Plastic, fiberglass and resin weigh little; terracotta and glazed ceramic add noticeably more; concrete or stone can weigh as much empty as the soil inside. Full saucers, water reservoirs and a standing watering can count too.

Point loads versus spread loads

Structural figures for balconies usually describe a load spread evenly over the whole area. Planters put a lot of weight on a small footprint.

Take a 15-gallon pot that weighs about 125 lb (57 kg) in total and stands on a base 18 in (46 cm) across. Its footprint is about 1.8 sq ft (0.16 m²), so that patch of floor carries about 70 lb per square foot (about 340 kg/m²). Spread over the whole balcony, the same pot would look trivial. Where it stands matters as much as how much it weighs.

So several medium pots spaced apart are kinder to a slab than one huge planter of the same total weight, and a row of heavy troughs along the outer edge concentrates load where many balconies are weakest.

Heavy items near the wall and over supports

You usually cannot tell from above how a balcony is built. Two common types behave differently:

  • Cantilevered balconies stick out from the building with no posts underneath. They are held only at the wall, so weight near the outer edge acts on that connection with the most leverage.
  • Supported balconies rest on posts, brackets, beams or hangers. Areas directly over a post or beam are typically stiffer than the middle of a span.

A rule for both types: heaviest items against the building wall, lighter ones toward the railing, nothing heavy on or over the railing. If you can see posts or beams, directly above a support is the next best spot. Railing boxes are a separate question, covered in railing planters.

Why design live loads are not spare capacity

Engineers design balconies for a "live load": the people, furniture and movable things a space should carry. In the US, ASCE 7 sets the minimum figures, and for balconies the value is tied to the use of the room the balcony serves. Canada, the UK and Australia have their own codes.

That figure is not an allowance to fill with planters:

  • It is already meant for people. A balcony full of guests is what it was designed for, and your pots share that budget.
  • It assumes the load is spread evenly. Pots are concentrated loads, as shown above.
  • Safety factors cover uncertainty in materials, workmanship and aging, not extra use.
  • Older buildings were designed to the codes of their day, and decades of weather can reduce what the structure can carry.
  • In cold climates, snow and ice sit on top of everything else, and a blocked drain can add standing water.

Add two adults, a small table and two chairs, and the balcony is carrying well over 600 lb (270 kg), with the single heaviest item sitting on less than 2 sq ft (0.2 m²).

None of this says whether that is fine; it gives someone qualified a list to check.

Warning signs of age and corrosion

Look from above and, if you can, from below:

  • Rust stains from the slab edge, railing posts or wall joint.
  • Cracked or flaking concrete, especially where steel shows.
  • A slab or deck sagging toward the outer edge.
  • Railings that move when pushed.
  • On timber balconies: soft or crumbly wood, corroded fasteners.
  • Water pooling for days, or cracks where the balcony meets the wall.
If you notice any of these, move heavy planters off the balcony, keep people off it and report it to the building manager in writing. Never test a balcony by loading it up, and never jump or bounce on it to check.

How to ask the building manager

A vague question gets a vague answer; a specific one reaches someone who can check it. Send, in writing:

  1. Your total planned weight, wet, and the weight of the heaviest single item with its footprint. The Pot Weight Planner data sheet lists both.
  2. Where each item will stand: against the wall, mid-balcony or at the railing.
  3. Whether anything goes on or over the railing.
  4. How pots are raised off the floor so drains and the waterproofing stay clear.

Ask about planter rules, any known load limit and the last structural inspection. Renters should check the lease first; balcony gardening for renters lists what to look for.

FAQ

How heavy is a large pot of wet soil?

A 15-gallon (57 L) pot of standard potting mix typically weighs 75 to 115 lb (34 to 51 kg) wet, before the pot. Garden soil would be heavier.

Is it safer to use many small pots than one big one?

For the slab, usually yes, because the weight is spread out. Small pots dry out faster, though.

Does lightweight potting mix solve the problem?

It reduces the weight, but a big pot is still heavy once watered, and light pots blow over more easily.
