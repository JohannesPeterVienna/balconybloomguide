Basil (Ocimum basilicum) is a warm-season annual herb that loves heat, sun and evenly moist mix – and hates cold nights, wind and wet leaves. On a sunny balcony one or two pots give fresh leaves from early summer to the first cool nights of fall, as long as you harvest correctly and never plant it out too early.

Light: how many sun hours?

University of Minnesota Extension recommends a sunny spot with at least six to eight hours of bright light a day. With fewer hours, basil grows soft and thin, tastes milder and is more prone to disease. On a balcony that usually means the railing or the front half of the floor. Check with the Sun & Shade Simulator: if the railing gets 6 hours or more from June to August, basil will do well there. Herbs that tolerate less sun – parsley, chives, mint – are covered in herbs for part shade; basil is not one of them.

When to put it out

Basil is more cold-sensitive than tomatoes. UMN advises waiting until after the last frost – mid to late May in Minnesota – before transplanting. Even above freezing, nights in the 40s °F (below about 10 °C) can blacken leaves and stall growth for weeks. On a balcony the air is often a little warmer than in the garden, but wind chills plants. Wait for settled warm nights, and keep supermarket pots indoors on a bright windowsill until then.

Pot size and weight

An established basil plant needs a pot at least 8 in (20 cm) across; a supermarket pot is actually many seedlings crowded together, and dividing it into three or four clumps in separate pots gives stronger plants. A 10 to 12 in (25 to 30 cm) pot takes a group of three plants.

The Pot Weight Planner gives about 8 L (2.2 gal) of mix for a 10 in round pot – about 14 lb (6 kg) wet in standard mix – and about 12.5 L and 21 lb (9.4 kg) for a 12 in pot. In a railing box, basil can go between other plants at about 8 to 10 in spacing, but the box must stay evenly moist.

Wind and heat

Basil leaves are large and soft. In wind they bruise, tear and lose water fast, and the plant stops growing. On a balcony the Wind Exposure Check rates Exposed or Very exposed, place basil behind a solid section of railing or in the lee of other pots, and use a slightly larger pot so it does not dry out between waterings. Heat is no problem as long as water keeps up: basil is a plant for hot balconies.

Watering

Keep the mix evenly moist but not soggy. Water at the base, in the morning, so the leaves stay dry – wet leaves overnight encourage disease. In summer heat, small pots need water daily; larger ones every one to two days. The Watering & Holiday Planner suggests checking every 1 to 2 days for an 8 in plastic pot in 6 hours of sun on warm days, and the same for a 10 in pot on hot days. Basil that wilts badly once usually recovers, but repeated drying out makes it bolt to flower and toughens the leaves.

Feeding

Feed lightly. UMN recommends a liquid feed every three to four weeks for container basil outdoors. Too much fertilizer gives fast, lush growth with less aroma. If you mixed slow-release fertilizer into the potting mix at planting, start liquid feeding in midsummer.

Harvest and pinching

Harvest from the top, not by picking single leaves from the bottom. Cut each stem just above a pair of leaves; two new shoots grow from that point and the plant becomes bushy. Start once the plant has three or four pairs of leaves, and never take more than about a third at once.

Pinch out flower spikes as soon as they appear. UMN notes that letting basil flower reduces the yield and can make the flavor bitter. For storage, freezing keeps basil better than air drying, which turns the leaves brown.

Downy mildew

The most serious basil disease in the US is basil downy mildew: leaves yellow between the veins and show a gray-purple fuzz on the underside, and whole plants can die. UMN and the Missouri Botanical Garden recommend resistant varieties (often marked DMR in catalogs), good air movement and dry leaves rather than sprays; products available to home gardeners are not effective. Remove infected plants promptly, bag them and do not compost them.

Winter by USDA zone

MBG lists sweet basil as an annual; it does not survive frost in any zone. UMN states plainly that basil cannot overwinter outdoors in Minnesota, and the same holds in practice across the country. A pot brought indoors before cold nights keeps going for a few weeks on a bright, warm windowsill, and rooted cuttings can be grown under a light – but in most homes winter basil is weak. Sow or buy new plants in spring.

Common mistakes

  • Putting it out too early. Cold nights stop growth for weeks.
  • Picking single leaves from the bottom. The plant gets tall and bare; cut above a leaf pair instead.
  • Letting it flower. Yield drops and leaves turn bitter.
  • Watering in the evening over the leaves. It invites downy mildew.
  • Keeping the supermarket pot as it is. Too many seedlings compete; divide it.

FAQ

Can basil grow with tomatoes in one pot?

Yes, a basil plant at the edge of a large tomato pot works, as long as the pot is big enough for both and stays moist.

Why does my basil turn black?

Usually cold: nights below about 50 °F (10 °C) damage the leaves. Downy mildew causes yellowing and dark patches with fuzz underneath.

Thai, lemon or Genovese basil?

All have the same needs. UMN mentions lemon and red-leaf types among those less susceptible to downy mildew.

Is basil safe around pets?

The ASPCA lists basil as non-toxic to cats and dogs.
