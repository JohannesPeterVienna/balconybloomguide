A hydrangea is the most water-hungry and the heaviest plant most people put on a balcony – and one of the most rewarding on an east-facing or partly shaded one. Bigleaf hydrangeas (Hydrangea macrophylla) give the familiar mophead and lacecap flowers but form next year's buds in late summer, so winter protection of the pot decides whether they flower. Panicle hydrangeas (H. paniculata) flower on new wood and are far hardier and more sun-tolerant.

Which hydrangea for a pot?

University of Minnesota Extension describes the main types by how they flower. Bigleaf hydrangeas bloom on old wood: the buds form in late summer to early fall for the following year, so a cold winter or late frost can kill them, and pruning in fall or spring removes them. Reblooming cultivars – the Endless Summer series and others – flower on both old and new wood and still give some flowers after a hard winter. Panicle hydrangeas such as 'Limelight' bloom on the current year's growth; UMN calls them hardy and reliable in cold climates, and they can be pruned in late winter or spring.

For a balcony that means: bigleaf hydrangeas for mild climates or for people who can move the pot into frost-free shelter; panicle hydrangeas, preferably compact cultivars, for cold climates and sunnier spots. Gift-shop hydrangeas sold in spring are often forced bigleaf types that are not reliably hardy.

Light: how many sun hours?

UMN notes that hydrangeas prefer morning sun and afternoon shade and should be kept out of hot, dry, exposed places. On a balcony, an east-facing exposure is ideal for a bigleaf hydrangea; a north-facing balcony with bright light also works, with fewer flowers. A south- or west-facing balcony is usually too hot for a bigleaf hydrangea in a pot, except deep under the slab where the afternoon sun does not reach.

Use the Sun & Shade Simulator to see when the sun reaches each spot: for a bigleaf hydrangea, aim for a spot that gets roughly 3 to 5 hours, all of them before early afternoon. The guide to east-facing balcony plants has companions for the same light.

Pot size and weight

Hydrangeas have dense roots and huge leaf area; a small pot means wilting every afternoon. Use at least 16 to 18 in (40 to 45 cm) for a mature shrub, and move up a size every two to three years. A pot with a large drainage hole and a moisture-retentive potting mix with some compost works best.

This is where weight becomes serious. The Pot Weight Planner gives about 49 L (12.9 gal) of mix for an 18 in round pot 16 in tall: about 81 lb (37 kg) wet in standard mix. A 20 in pot holds about 66 L, around 110 lb (50 kg). Glazed ceramic or concrete adds 30 lb or more. Put large hydrangeas against the building wall or on a structural edge your building manager names, consider a plastic or fiberglass pot, and read balcony weight limits before buying.

Wind and heat

The large, soft leaves lose water fast in wind and tear in gusts. On a balcony the Wind Exposure Check rates Exposed or Very exposed, a bigleaf hydrangea will struggle; choose a compact panicle hydrangea, keep it against the wall, or grow grasses there instead. In heat, hydrangeas often wilt in the afternoon even with moist roots and recover by evening. Daily wilting despite moist mix is a sign the spot gets too much afternoon sun.

Watering

Hydrangeas need a lot of water and should never dry out completely. In summer, water large pots deeply every day or two and check small ones twice a day in a heat wave. The Watering & Holiday Planner estimates around 1.5 L a day for a large plant in an 18 in pot in 4 hours of sun on hot days; in practice, measure the drainage – water until some runs out. For a holiday, a large saucer or a self-watering pot is almost essential.

Rainwater or softer water is an advantage, especially for blue-flowered bigleaf hydrangeas.

Feeding and flower color

Feed from spring to midsummer with a fertilizer for flowering shrubs, at the label rate; stop by late summer so new growth can harden before winter. Too much nitrogen gives leaves instead of flowers.

Bigleaf hydrangea flower color depends on soil chemistry: blue needs acidic mix with available aluminum, pink appears in neutral to alkaline conditions; white cultivars stay white. In a pot you control the mix: an acidic potting mix and a hydrangea "bluing" product containing aluminum sulfate keep flowers blue, while hard, alkaline tap water slowly pushes them back towards pink. MBG notes for 'Mariesii' that flower color shifts with soil pH.

Overwintering by USDA zone

MBG gives zones 6 to 9 for many bigleaf cultivars, with some reblooming cultivars rated to zone 4 or 5; UMN considers bigleaf hydrangeas hardy only to zone 5 in the ground. Panicle hydrangeas are much hardier. Penn State Extension's rule for pots is to plan for two zones colder. Check your zone on the USDA map.

  • Bigleaf, zones 8 and warmer: leave the pot out, sheltered against the house wall, with the pot wrapped.
  • Bigleaf, zones 6 and 7: after leaf fall, move the pot into an unheated, frost-free or only lightly freezing space – a garage or shed – or group and heavily insulate it outdoors. Water rarely, keeping the root ball barely moist.
  • Bigleaf, zone 5 and colder: an unheated garage or shed is the practical option; outdoors the flower buds rarely survive.
  • Panicle: in zones 5 and warmer the pot can usually stay out, grouped and wrapped; colder, shelter it.

Do not prune bigleaf hydrangeas in fall or early spring; remove only dead tips once buds swell. See overwintering balcony plants.

Common mistakes

  • Afternoon sun. Daily wilting and scorched leaves.
  • A pot that is too small. Water runs out by midday.
  • Pruning a bigleaf hydrangea in spring. It removes the flower buds.
  • Leaving the pot fully exposed in a cold winter. Buds freeze; the plant lives but does not flower.
  • Ignoring weight. A large hydrangea in ceramic can exceed 150 lb.

FAQ

Why did my hydrangea not flower this year?

For bigleaf types, the usual causes are frozen buds in winter, pruning at the wrong time or too much shade.

Can a hydrangea stay in a pot permanently?

Yes, with a large enough pot, repotting every few years and winter protection.

Are hydrangeas toxic to pets?

The ASPCA lists hydrangea as toxic to cats and dogs.
