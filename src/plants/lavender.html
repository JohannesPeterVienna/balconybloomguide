Lavender is a sun plant for the hottest spot on the balcony. English lavender (Lavandula angustifolia) is a small evergreen shrub from dry, stony Mediterranean slopes: it wants long hours of direct sun, a gritty mix that drains in seconds and very little fertilizer. In a pot it dies far more often from wet roots in winter than from drought in summer.

Light: how many sun hours?

Lavender needs full sun: at least 6 hours of direct sun in summer, and the flowering and fragrance improve with every extra hour. With less light the plants become open and floppy, flower sparsely and are more prone to rot because the mix stays wet longer.

On most balconies only the railing reaches those numbers. Use the Sun & Shade Simulator and look at the railing row for June: south-, southwest- and west-facing balconies often give 7 to 10 hours there, an east-facing balcony usually 5 to 7. If the middle of the floor gets fewer than 6 hours, put lavender in a pot at the very front rather than against the wall. The guide to south-facing balconies lists other plants for the same spot.

Pot size, mix and weight

A single young lavender grows well in a pot about 12 in (30 cm) across; a mature 'Hidcote' or 'Munstead' (MBG gives about 12 to 20 in tall) is happier in 14 in (35 cm). A larger pot is not automatically better: lots of mix around a small root ball stays wet. Unglazed terracotta suits lavender because water evaporates through the clay.

The mix matters more than the size. Use a potting mix with a third or more of coarse grit, pumice or perlite, and do not put the pot in a saucer that holds water. Illinois Extension suggests a potting mix without added fertilizer for containers.

According to the Pot Weight Planner, a 14 in round pot, 12 in tall, holds about 22 L (5.8 gal) of mix. Wet, that is about 29 lb (13 kg) of lightweight mix, 36 lb (16 kg) of standard mix or more for a gritty mix, which is heavier per litre. Terracotta adds roughly another 15 to 20 lb (7 to 9 kg). A row of lavender pots along the railing adds up quickly – check the total before you buy and read how much weight a balcony can hold.

Wind and heat

Lavender is one of the plants that cope with an exposed balcony. The narrow gray leaves lose little water in wind, and compact cultivars stay upright. On balconies the Wind Exposure Check rates Very exposed, use a wide, heavy pot so it cannot tip, and expect the flower spikes to bend. Reflected heat from a light wall or a glass railing is no problem for an established plant; a newly planted one still needs regular water in its first summer.

Watering

Water deeply, then let the top of the mix dry before watering again. Established lavender is drought-tolerant in the ground, but a pot holds only a few litres, so on hot summer days a pot in full sun may still need water every two to four days. In the Watering & Holiday Planner, a 14 in terracotta pot with a compact plant in 8 hours of sun on hot days comes out at "check every 2–3 days". Always check first: if the mix is still damp an inch down, wait.

In winter water only enough to keep the root ball from drying out completely. Most potted lavender that dies in winter dies from wet, cold roots.

Feeding and pruning

Feed little or not at all. Fresh potting mix usually contains enough nutrients for the first season; after that, a light dose of slow-release fertilizer in spring is plenty. Rich feeding gives soft, leafy growth that flowers less and suffers more in winter.

Prune every year, or the plant turns woody and bare in the middle. After flowering, cut off the spent flower stems and trim the leafy shoots lightly. In spring, when new growth appears, shorten the shoots again but do not cut back into old, leafless wood – lavender rarely regrows from it.

Overwintering by USDA zone

MBG gives English lavender as hardy in USDA zones 5 to 8 when planted in the ground, and notes that cultivars differ; Illinois Extension adds that even plants labelled for zone 5 can be damaged in harsh winters. Spanish and French lavenders (L. stoechas, L. dentata) are much less hardy and are best treated as tender plants outside the mildest zones.

A pot is colder than the ground. Penn State Extension advises choosing container plants rated about two zones colder than your own, because roots in a pot above ground can get almost as cold as the air. In practice:

  • Zones 8 and warmer: English lavender can stay outside in its pot. Keep it out of winter rain if you can.
  • Zones 6 and 7: move the pot against the house wall, out of rain and wind, raise it on pot feet and wrap the pot (not the plant) with burlap or bubble wrap. Bring terracotta into shelter in hard frost so it does not crack.
  • Zone 5 and colder: keep the pot over winter in an unheated but frost-protected, bright place such as a cold stairwell or garage with a window, and water rarely.

Lavender is not a houseplant; a warm living room in winter weakens it. See overwintering balcony plants for wrapping and grouping pots.

Common mistakes

  • A saucer full of water. The roots rot within weeks. Use pot feet.
  • Too little sun. Five hours or less gives lanky plants and few flowers.
  • Fertilizing like a petunia. Lavender needs only a fraction of the feed.
  • Cutting into bare wood. Prune lightly every year instead of hard every few years.
  • Buying "lavender" without a name. Tender Spanish lavender sold in spring often dies in a zone 6 winter; check the botanical name.

FAQ

Which lavender is best for pots?

Compact English lavenders such as 'Hidcote' and 'Munstead' stay small enough for a 12 to 14 in pot and are among the hardiest.

Can lavender grow on an east-facing balcony?

If the railing gets about 6 hours of morning sun in summer, yes, but it flowers less than on a south or west balcony. Check the numbers in the simulator first.

Does lavender keep mosquitoes away?

There is no good evidence that a lavender plant on the balcony repels mosquitoes; grow it for the scent, the flowers and the bees.

Is lavender safe around pets?

The ASPCA lists lavender as toxic to dogs, cats and horses; eating it can cause nausea and vomiting. Keep pots out of reach of pets that chew plants.
