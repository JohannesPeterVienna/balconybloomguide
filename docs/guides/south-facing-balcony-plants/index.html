Heat & orientation

South-Facing Balcony Plants That Handle Heat

A south-facing balcony gets the most direct sun of any orientation, which suits Mediterranean herbs, succulents, chilies and other heat lovers. The catch is heat and water: walls, floors and glass add reflected and stored heat, and pots dry fast. Less obvious is that in midsummer the balcony above shades the back of the floor at noon, so the railing, not the back wall, is the hot spot.

Why the railing gets more sun than the back wall in summer

At solar noon a south-facing balcony looks straight at the sun. How high the sun stands then depends on your latitude and the season: roughly 90 degrees minus your latitude, plus about 23 degrees at the June solstice, minus about 23 degrees at the December solstice. Around 45 degrees north, which runs close to Minneapolis, Montreal and Portland, Oregon, that gives a noon sun of about 68 degrees in June and about 22 degrees in December.

A high sun cannot reach far under the slab above. On a balcony 5 ft (1.5 m) deep with the slab 8 ft (2.4 m) above the floor, the June noon sun lands only about 3 ft (1 m) in from the railing. The back of the floor and the whole back wall sit in shade at the very time the railing gets the strongest sun of the year. In winter it is the other way round: the low sun reaches the back wall and warms it.

Further south, at 35 degrees north, the June noon sun stands even higher and the shaded strip at the back gets wider. Further north, as in the UK at around 51 to 55 degrees, the summer sun is lower and reaches deeper. Away from noon the sun swings to the side, and the slab's shadow reaches further forward. The Sun & Shade Simulator works all of this out by month for your own location, depth, ceiling height and railing type.

Check this on your balcony: at solar noon, which on daylight saving time is roughly 1 p.m., put a strip of tape on the floor along the edge of the slab's shadow. Do it again a month later. The tape marks show exactly where the summer sun-and-shade boundary runs on your balcony.

Heat from walls, floors and glass

Plants on a south balcony sit in a box of surfaces that absorb, reflect and store heat:

  • Floors and walls. Concrete, stone and dark tiles heat up in direct sun and radiate that heat for hours, well into the evening. Pots standing directly on a hot floor heat from below.
  • Glass railings. Clear glass lets sun reach plants on the floor right behind it, which a solid railing would shade. Glass and shiny surfaces also reflect light, so plants near them can get a second dose.
  • Windows and pale walls nearby. Reflections from your own or neighboring windows can put extra sun on part of the balcony at certain times of day, even in spots the geometry says should be shaded.

So the railing zone behind a glass railing is often the hottest spot on the balcony, while the back wall can suit part-sun plants in July and become a sun trap in winter.

Water demand: the real limit on a south balcony

A plant in full sun moves a lot of water through its leaves, and a pot holds only a small reserve. In a summer heat wave, small pots in full sun on a south balcony may need water every day, sometimes twice. What helps most:

  • Size. Larger pots buffer heat and hold more water. Long-season crops such as tomatoes need large containers; see tomatoes on a balcony for sizes.
  • Material. Unglazed terracotta loses water through its walls; glazed ceramic, plastic and fiberglass dry more slowly.
  • An air gap. Pot feet or a slatted stand keep pots off a hot floor.
  • A reservoir. Self-watering containers bridge hot days and short trips.

Bigger, wetter pots are also heavier. Before you fill a balcony with large containers, add up the weights with the Pot Weight Planner and ask your building manager what the balcony is designed for.

The watering planner estimates how fast each pot dries; for pots at the front, enter the railing hours, not the back-wall hours.

Decision rules for a south balcony

  1. Railing, summer: only plants rated for full sun and heat. If the railing is glass, add a margin: choose the toughest ones there.
  2. Back wall, summer: check your tape marks. If it is shaded around noon, part-sun plants such as those in the east-facing guide can work there.
  3. Back wall, winter: the low sun makes it the warmest spot on the balcony, a good place for tender plants you overwinter outside in mild climates.
  4. Any pot that wilts daily: move it back, give it a bigger pot or replace it with a succulent.

In the Southern Hemisphere, all of this applies to north-facing balconies instead; there, a south-facing balcony is the shady one.

FAQ

Is a south-facing balcony too hot for plants?

Not for heat-tolerant ones. It is too hot for shade lovers such as fuchsias and hostas at the railing, though these can sometimes manage at the back wall of a deep balcony with a slab above.

Why do plants at my back wall get less sun than expected?

In summer the noon sun is high, and the slab above blocks it from the back of the balcony. The deeper the balcony and the lower the ceiling, the bigger that shaded strip.

Do glass railings make a balcony hotter?

They let more sun reach the floor right behind them than a solid railing would, and they reflect some light, so plants close to them usually get more light and heat.
