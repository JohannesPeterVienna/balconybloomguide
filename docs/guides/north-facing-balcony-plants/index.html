North-Facing Balcony Plants (Shade-Tolerant)

A north-facing balcony gets little or no direct sun for most of the year; around midsummer, an open one may catch low sun for a while after sunrise and before sunset. Grow plants chosen for foliage and bright shade, such as hostas, ferns, fatsia and ivy, and put the ones that need the most light at the railing, where they see the most sky, not against the back wall.

What north-facing light actually is

Light on a north balcony is mostly skylight: sunlight scattered by the sky and reflected from buildings opposite. It is soft, cool and even, with no midday glare. For plants, the question is not how many hours of sun a spot gets but how much open sky it can see.

Direct sun is a seasonal bonus. Between the spring and fall equinoxes, the sun rises north of due east and sets north of due west. That means an unobstructed north wall can see the sun for a while after sunrise and before sunset, most of all around the June solstice. That sun is low and strikes the wall at a glancing angle, so side walls, neighboring buildings and trees often remove most of it. From October to February, at mid-northern latitudes, a north wall gets no direct sun at all.

This is why the Sun & Shade Simulator often puts a north balcony in the shade class, under 3 hours of direct sun, for most months, even if you remember the sun reaching your chair on a June evening. Try your own balcony there; the month view shows the short summer window clearly.

Why the balcony above matters even more here

On a sunny balcony, the slab above blocks sun. On a north balcony it blocks sky, which is your main light source. A plant at the railing sees a wide sweep of sky, from the horizon almost straight up. A plant at the back wall of a deep balcony sees only the slice between the horizon and the edge of the slab.

A worked example: on a balcony 6 ft (1.8 m) deep with the slab 8 ft (2.4 m) above the floor, a pot at the back wall sees sky from the horizon up to about 53 degrees. If a building across the street rises to about 30 degrees above your horizon, that pot sees only the band between 30 and 53 degrees. The same pot moved to the railing sees from 30 degrees to nearly overhead, a band of sky more than twice as tall. On a north balcony, a few feet of floor can be the difference between a plant that grows and one that slowly stretches and fades.

Check this on your balcony: the shadow test

Around midday on a bright day, hold your hand about 12 in (30 cm) above a sheet of white paper at each spot: back wall, middle and railing.

  • Crisp, dark shadow: direct sun is reaching the spot. Note the time; on a north balcony this usually only happens early or late in summer.
  • Soft but clearly visible shadow: bright shade. Most plants in the table below are happy here.
  • Faint shadow or none: deep shade. Only ferns, ivy and fatsia are worth trying, and none of them will flower much.

A second check: sit at each spot at plant height and look out. If you have to duck to see any sky at all, that spot is deep shade, whatever the label on the plant says.

You can add light without adding sun. A pale back wall and a light-colored floor bounce skylight back onto the plants. Raising pots on a plant stand or a rail box lifts them into a wider view of the sky; see railing planters for safe sizes and fixings.

Shade-tolerant plants for north balconies

Expect foliage first and flowers second. Pale and variegated leaves and white flowers read well in low light; dark purple leaves tend to disappear.

Water and winter on a north balcony

Pots in shade dry slowly, so overwatering is the more common mistake. Use pots with drainage holes, empty saucers after rain, and water only when the top inch (2.5 cm) of mix feels dry. The watering planner will usually show much longer intervals for a north balcony than for the same pots facing south.

Winter is the other challenge. A north balcony gets no winter sun to thaw frozen pots, so the mix can stay frozen for long spells. Roots in a pot are far more exposed than roots in the ground, and evergreens such as fatsia and ivy can dry out in winter wind while their roots cannot take up water. For plants that stay out all year, a common rule of thumb is to choose ones hardy to about two zones colder than yours. The overwintering guide covers insulation and moving pots.

In the Southern Hemisphere the picture flips: a north-facing balcony is the sunny one, and this guide applies to south-facing balconies instead.

FAQ

Does a north-facing balcony get any sun?

In the Northern Hemisphere, only in the warmer half of the year, early in the morning and late in the evening, and only if side walls and neighboring buildings do not block it. In winter, none.

Can anything flower on a north-facing balcony?

Yes. Hellebores in late winter, wishbone flower and impatiens in summer, and fuchsias at the railing of a bright north balcony. Expect fewer flowers than in sun.

Can I grow vegetables on a north-facing balcony?

Leafy greens such as lettuce can manage at the railing of a bright one. Fruiting crops like tomatoes and peppers need far more direct sun than a north balcony gives.

Why are my plants stretching and pale?

They are not getting enough light. Move them toward the railing, lift them higher, or swap them for one of the deep-shade plants above.
