Sun & orientation

West-Facing Balcony Plants

A west-facing balcony gets direct sun from early afternoon until sunset, which is exactly when the air is hottest and pots are already drier from the morning. Choose plants that tolerate heat and drying wind, give them larger pots than you think, and keep the delicate ones toward the back wall, where the balcony above cuts the hours short.

Why afternoon sun is harder than morning sun

On paper, five hours of sun on a west balcony and five hours on an east balcony are the same. For a plant in a pot they are not. Three effects stack up in the afternoon:

  • Air temperature. On a clear summer day the air usually peaks in mid to late afternoon. A west balcony gets its strongest sun right then, so leaves have to deal with direct sunlight and hot air at the same time.
  • Stored heat. Concrete, brick and stone walls and floors have been warming since noon. After sunset they keep giving that heat back, so a west balcony often stays warm well into the evening.
  • Dry pots. By afternoon, a pot watered in the morning has already lost part of its water. Small pots can run dry before the sun even leaves.

The result is a pattern many west-balcony gardeners know: plants look fine at noon, wilt at 5 p.m. and recover overnight. Occasional evening wilting in a heat wave is not always a watering emergency, but daily wilting means the pot is too small, the plant is wrong for the spot, or both.

A west balcony's day, hour by hour

A wall facing due west sees the sun from solar noon until sunset. On daylight saving time, solar noon usually falls somewhere around 1 p.m. on the clock, and in June sunset can come after 8 p.m. in much of the US, Canada and Europe, and after 9 p.m. in the UK and northern Europe. The balcony above you shortens the day at the back: while the sun is still high in early afternoon, it shines past the slab edge onto the railing but not deeper in.

Notice what the diagram shows: even the back wall, the "shady" spot, gets several hours of direct sun, and they are the hot ones. On a west balcony, sun hours alone understate the stress. Run your own balcony through the Sun & Shade Simulator, then apply the rule below.

Decision rule: count the direct sun hours after 1 p.m. in July. If a spot gets 4 or more of them, treat it as a hot full-sun spot and choose heat-tolerant plants, even if the total would only rate as part sun. If a spot gets fewer than 2, plants that like part shade will usually be fine there.

Wind: the second west-side problem

In much of North America and Europe, the prevailing winds come from the west or southwest, so a west-facing balcony is often also the windy side of the building. Local effects can override that: corners, gaps between buildings and higher floors all funnel wind. Wind matters on a hot balcony because it strips moisture from leaves and pots, and it makes tall plants and light pots unstable.

Practical consequences for a west balcony:

  • Prefer plants with small, tough, gray or narrow leaves (lavender, grasses, succulents). They lose less water than large, soft leaves.
  • Use pots wide at the base and heavy enough not to tip, but check the total load first; a wet pot is much heavier than a dry one.
  • Keep tall plants out of the corners, where gusts are strongest.

The Wind Exposure Check gives your balcony a wind class, and the windy balcony guide covers fixings and pot choice in detail.

Never stand pots or loose planters on the top of the railing or a ledge, and fix rail boxes on the inside of the railing where your building allows it. Afternoon gusts and summer storms can lift or tip containers, and anything falling from a balcony can seriously injure someone below.

Taking the edge off a west balcony

You cannot change the orientation, but you can soften it:

  • Bigger pots. More mix holds more water and heats up more slowly. Moving a plant from a small pot to one roughly twice the width often turns daily wilting into a plant that lasts the day.
  • Light-colored pots. Dark pots in direct sun get noticeably hotter, which stresses roots near the pot wall.
  • Group pots. Pots standing together shade each other's sides.
  • Afternoon shade where you need it. A shade cloth or awning on the railing side cuts the hottest hours, but check your lease or building rules first.
  • Water early. Water in the morning so the mix is full when the sun arrives; in heat waves, check again in the early evening.

The watering planner estimates how fast each pot dries from its size, material, sun hours and wind, which is useful on a west balcony where small pots dry much faster than large ones.

In the Southern Hemisphere a west balcony still gets the hot afternoon sun, but the sun arcs through the north, so a side wall on the north side, rather than the south side, is the one that shades it in the early afternoon.

FAQ

Is a west-facing balcony good for plants?

Yes, for the right plants. It suits heat lovers such as geraniums, lantana and stonecrop. It is a poor choice for plants that need cool roots and steady moisture, such as fuchsias or astilbes, unless they sit far back.

Is west-facing sun stronger than south-facing?

Not stronger in intensity, but it arrives when the air is hottest and lasts until sunset in summer, so plants often feel more heat stress on a west balcony than the hour count suggests.

Should I water in the evening on a west balcony?

Morning is better as a routine. An extra evening check during heat waves is fine; water the mix rather than the leaves.
