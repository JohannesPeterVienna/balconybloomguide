Balcony Calendar: Monthly Tasks for All 12 Months

This calendar lists the main balcony jobs for each month in a temperate Northern Hemisphere climate, roughly USDA zones 6–8 and most of the UK (USDA 8–9). The two dates that matter most are your average last spring frost and first fall frost; everything else hangs on them. Shift the months to match your own climate and balcony, as explained at the end.

How to read this calendar

Find your average last and first frost dates from your local extension service, national weather service or gardening organization. In the temperate climate this calendar assumes, the last spring frost typically falls somewhere between late March and mid-May, and the first fall frost between late September and November. Tender plants go out after the last frost and come in before the first.

Your balcony can run a little ahead of or behind the open-ground dates. A south-facing balcony against a heated wall and under a ceiling often stays a degree or two warmer on frosty nights. A windy, open balcony on a high floor or a north-facing one with little sun warms up later in spring. The Sun & Shade Simulator shows how direct sun on your railing and back wall changes from month to month, which tells you when sun-loving crops will actually get enough light.

Each season below has a table with the key tasks, what to plant or sow, and what to watch for. For detail on single topics, follow the links to the matching guides.

Spring: March to May

Spring is about timing. Sow hardy crops early, start tender ones indoors, and wait for the last frost before anything tender goes out.

Hardening off means putting seedlings outside for a few hours a day, in shade and out of the wind at first, then gradually longer. On a windy balcony, give it more days rather than fewer. For tomato container sizes and sun needs, see tomatoes on a balcony.

Summer: June to August

In summer the job shifts from planting to keeping up: watering, feeding, tying in and harvesting. Small pots in full sun may need water every day.

Fall: September to November

Fall is for planting next spring's flowers and preparing for frost. Most of the work happens in a few weeks around the first frost date.

Fall balcony tasks (temperate Northern Hemisphere)
MonthKey tasksPlant or sowWatch out for
SeptemberClear spent summer crops, refresh mix in emptied pots, check plants for pests before they come indoorsDaffodils (Narcissus) and crocuses in pots; pansies, violas, heathers, ornamental cabbage and kaleCooler nights; first frost in colder areas
OctoberBring geraniums, fuchsias, citrus and other tender plants in before the first frost. Move hardy pots from the railing to the back wallTulips (Tulipa) in pots, garlic cloves in deep containersFirst frost; fallen leaves clogging the balcony drain
NovemberWrap pots that stay out, raise them on pot feet, empty and store saucers, drain and store hosesLast chance for tulips while the mix is still workableFall storms: secure or remove anything a gust could lift

Tulips and daffodils are toxic to cats and dogs, the bulbs most of all, so keep pots of them where pets cannot dig. The overwintering guide explains which plants can stay out and how to protect them.

Winter: December to February

Winter on a balcony is quiet but not empty. Plants under a ceiling get no rain or snow, so they still need occasional water, and stored plants indoors need checking.

Winter balcony tasks (temperate Northern Hemisphere)
MonthKey tasksPlant or sowWatch out for
DecemberWater evergreens on mild days when the mix is not frozen, brush heavy snow off planters, check wraps after windNothing outdoors; plan next year's planting by sun hours per spotSnow load on open balconies; de-icing salt near pots
JanuaryCheck stored tender plants for pests and dryness every couple of weeks, keep watering winter pots occasionallyIndoors under strong light: chili peppers, which need a long seasonFrozen saucers, wind burn on evergreens, pests on indoor plants
FebruaryCut back overwintered geraniums and fuchsias, order seeds and supplies, check that pots have survived without cracksIndoors: sweet peppers and eggplants about 8–10 weeks before last frostA warm spell that tempts you to plant out too early

Winter is the best time to measure your balcony: depth, ceiling height, railing type and the sun on each spot. A plan made now avoids buying plants in April that your balcony cannot support, whether because of sun, wind or weight.

Adjusting for your climate and hemisphere

Use your own last and first frost dates as anchors and slide the tables to fit.

  • Colder climates (roughly USDA zones 3–5, inland and high-altitude areas): spring tasks often move two to four weeks later, fall tasks earlier, and the outdoor season for tender plants is shorter. Choose fast-maturing and compact varieties.
  • Warmer climates (roughly USDA zones 9–10, mild coastal areas): spring starts several weeks earlier and frost may barely occur. Midsummer heat can be the hard season instead of winter; many leafy greens grow best from fall to spring, and balconies facing west or south may need shade in July and August.
  • Southern Hemisphere (Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, South America): move every month by six months, so March in this calendar becomes September, and October becomes April. Orientation flips as well: there, a north-facing balcony gets the most sun and a south-facing one the least.

Within any climate, your balcony's position matters. If your railing gets full sun but the back wall sits in shade for much of spring, plant the early crops at the railing and keep the back wall for shade-tolerant plants; the north-facing balcony guide has options for the shady side.

FAQ

When is it safe to put tomatoes and basil out?

After your average last frost date, once nights stay above about 50 °F (10 °C). Planting earlier rarely gains anything, because cold slows their growth.

Can I plant anything on a balcony in winter?

Hardy plants in frost-proof pots, such as heathers, some evergreens and pansies in milder areas, can be planted when the mix is not frozen. Most sowing waits for spring or happens indoors.

Why does my balcony seem a month behind the calendar?

A north-facing, shaded or very windy balcony warms up later than open ground in your area. Shift the dates by your own observations and give crops the sunniest spot you have.
