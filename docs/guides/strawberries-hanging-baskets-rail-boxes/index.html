Strawberries in Hanging Baskets and Rail Boxes

For hanging baskets and railing boxes, choose everbearing or day-neutral strawberries: they fruit in several flushes from early summer into fall instead of all at once. Plant three plants in a 12 in (30 cm) basket or three to four in a 24 in (60 cm) rail box, give them at least 6 hours of sun, and expect a hanging basket to need water every day in summer. On a part-shade balcony, alpine strawberries are the better bet.

Which strawberry for which spot

Garden strawberries (Fragaria × ananassa) are sold in three groups, and the difference matters more in a pot than in a bed.

  • June-bearing (short-day) strawberries give one large crop over two to three weeks in early summer and then put their energy into runners. In a small container that means one short harvest and a tangle of runners for the rest of the season.
  • Everbearing strawberries usually crop in two or three flushes: early summer, midsummer and late summer to fall. They make fewer runners.
  • Day-neutral strawberries form flowers regardless of day length and keep fruiting as long as temperatures are moderate. Flowering slows in hot spells and picks up again when it cools. They are the best match for a container that you want to look and eat from all summer.

Alpine strawberries (Fragaria vesca, usually sold as alpine or woodland strawberries) are a different species: small plants, small aromatic berries, and many varieties make few or no runners. They tolerate part shade better than garden strawberries and fruit for a long season, which makes them the answer for a balcony with only 3 to 5 hours of direct sun. Expect handfuls, not bowls.

Sun and placement on the balcony

Garden strawberries need about 6 hours or more of direct sun for sweet fruit. On most balconies that means the railing, because the ceiling above shades the back of the floor when the sun is high, and a solid railing shades anything standing on the floor behind it. A box on the inside of the railing or a basket hung near the front edge gets the most light. The Sun & Shade Simulator gives clear-sky hours for railing, middle and back wall so you can decide between garden and alpine strawberries before buying.

East- and west-facing balconies both work; a west-facing rail box in a heat wave needs extra water in the afternoon. For the general orientation pattern, see the guides to east-facing and west-facing balconies (Southern Hemisphere readers: swap north and south).

Why hanging baskets dry out so fast

A hanging basket is the hardest container on the balcony to keep watered. It holds little mix, it is exposed to air and sun on every side, and it hangs where the wind is strongest. On a hot, windy day a 12 in basket in full sun can go from wet to dry within a day. Strawberries react badly: dry spells mean small, hard berries and stressed plants, and day-neutral types stop flowering sooner.

  • Water until it drips from the bottom, then check again in the evening during heat waves.
  • Choose a basket with a solid liner or a built-in reservoir rather than an open wire frame with a thin liner.
  • Hang baskets where you can reach them easily, or fit a pulley so you can lower them for watering.
  • Think about where the drips land. On a balcony, the water runs onto the floor below or onto a neighbor's balcony; a drip tray or reservoir basket helps.

The Watering Rhythm & Holiday Planner compares how fast your basket dries compared with your larger pots, which is useful before a weekend away.

A watered 14 in (35 cm) basket can weigh around 20 lb (9 kg). Hang it from a hook or bracket rated for that weight, and never hang baskets on the outside of the railing over a street, path or the balcony below.

Mix, feeding and pollination

Use a fresh, free-draining potting mix; do not reuse old mix from a tomato pot, as the two share some soil-borne diseases. Once flowering starts, a liquid feed for fruiting plants every one to two weeks keeps everbearing types producing, since a small basket runs out of nutrients quickly.

Strawberries are largely self-fertile, but visiting insects help the flower set evenly shaped fruit. Poorly pollinated flowers give small, lumpy berries. On a high floor or an enclosed balcony with few bees, brush each open flower lightly with a small soft paintbrush every day or two.

Birds and other thieves

Birds find ripe strawberries on balconies too, especially blackbirds, pigeons and starlings in the UK and robins and mockingbirds in North America. A loose, fine net over the basket or box, held off the fruit by a few short canes, works well. Pick berries as soon as they color fully instead of leaving them for another day. Hanging baskets have one advantage: slugs and snails rarely reach them.

Overwintering in containers

Garden strawberries are hardy in the ground across most of USDA zones 4 to 8, but roots in a container are far more exposed than roots in the ground, because cold reaches the pot from every side. Where winters are mild, plants can stay out with no protection. Where the mix freezes solid for long periods, take baskets down, group pots against the house wall, wrap them in burlap or bubble wrap, or move them to an unheated, sheltered spot. Keep the mix barely moist, never wet, and remove dead leaves in early spring. Our overwintering guide explains the general methods.

Strawberry plants are most productive in their first two years. Replace them every two to three years, or root a few runners in small pots in late summer as replacements.

FAQ

Can I grow strawberries on a north-facing balcony?

Garden strawberries will grow but crop poorly. Alpine strawberries are the realistic choice if the spot gets around 3 to 5 hours of direct sun.

Should I cut off the runners?

Yes, on most containers. Runners take energy from fruiting. Keep one or two only if you want new plants.

Why are my berries small and dry?

Usually drought between waterings, an exhausted mix or too many plants in a small basket. Water daily in heat and feed regularly once flowering starts.
