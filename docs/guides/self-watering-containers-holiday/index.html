Self-Watering Containers for Balcony Holidays

A self-watering container can keep a balcony plant going for several days to about two weeks, depending on reservoir size, plant size, sun and wind. Wicks and drip spikes cover shorter trips. Whatever you use, do a trial run at home first, because the only reliable number is the one your own balcony gives you.

How a self-watering container works

A self-watering (sub-irrigated) container has two layers. At the bottom is a water reservoir. Above it sits a perforated platform that holds the potting mix. One part of the mix, or a separate wick, reaches down through the platform into the water. Capillary action pulls water up from there into the root zone as the mix dries, so the plant draws water at the rate it actually uses it.

Three details decide whether the design works:

  • Fill tube. A pipe from the top of the pot down to the reservoir lets you refill without wetting the surface.
  • Wicking zone. A column of mix or a wick that touches the water. If it is too small or the mix is too coarse, water does not rise far enough.
  • Overflow hole. A hole at the top edge of the reservoir, just below the platform. Excess water drains out here instead of flooding the roots. Without it, a heavy rain on an open balcony turns the pot into a bog.

Wicks, drip spikes and grouping

If your pots have no reservoir, you can add one from outside.

Wick from a bucket. Push one end of a soaked cord a few inches into the root zone and put the other end in a water container set beside the pot, at about the same height as the soil surface or a little above. Synthetic cord (nylon or acrylic) lasts longer than cotton, which tends to rot. One wick suits a small pot; a large container needs two or three. Thin wicks deliver less water than a thirsty plant in full sun uses, so they work best for herbs, small flowering plants and pots in shade.

Drip bottles and spikes. A terracotta or plastic spike pushed into the mix, with an upturned bottle in it, releases water as the soil around it dries. Delivery depends on how firmly the soil sits around the spike, so two identical setups can run dry days apart.

Grouping in shade. This costs nothing and helps with every other method. Move pots off the railing and against the back wall, out of the afternoon sun, and set them close together so they shade each other's pots and keep the air around them a little more humid. Under a deep balcony ceiling, the back wall often gets a fraction of the direct sun the railing gets; the Sun & Shade Simulator shows how large that difference is on your balcony. On a high floor, the back wall is usually also the least windy spot.

How long each method lasts

The ranges below are rough orientation values for a warm summer week, not guarantees. A large tomato in full sun on a windy railing can empty a reservoir that would keep a geranium in shade going for two weeks.

Do a test run before the trip

A week or two before you leave, set everything up exactly as you will leave it and stop watering from the top. Then measure:

  1. Fill the reservoir or bucket to a marked line.
  2. Check the level at the same time each day for three or four days.
  3. Note the daily drop and the weather on those days.

Worked example. A self-watering box has a reservoir 2 in (5 cm) deep. During a sunny test, the water level drops about 0.5 in (1.3 cm) a day. That gives roughly 4 days at test-run weather. If a heat wave is forecast for your trip, assume faster use and plan for 3 days, or move the box to the back wall and test again. If the test shows only 4 days and you will be gone for 10, you need a bigger reservoir, a second water source, or a neighbor with a key.

Also check the finger test during the trial. If the top 1–2 in (2.5–5 cm) of mix is bone dry but the reservoir is still full, the wick is not lifting enough water and the roots are not getting it.

Water is heavy: 1 gallon (3.8 L) weighs about 8.3 lb (3.8 kg). A row of filled reservoirs and buckets adds noticeable load on top of the wet soil. Add them to your list in the Pot Weight Planner and read how much weight a balcony can hold before you line up extra water containers.

Checklist for the last two days

  • Water every pot thoroughly from the top until water runs out of the drainage holes, then refill all reservoirs.
  • Pick off open flowers and ripe fruit; a tomato with ten ripening fruits drinks more than one that has just been harvested.
  • Cover bare soil with a thin layer of mulch to cut evaporation from the surface.
  • Move pots from the railing to the back wall and group them. Secure anything a gust could tip over, especially bottles on spikes.
  • Check whether rain can reach the pots. Under a balcony ceiling it usually cannot, so do not count on it. On an open top-floor balcony, check that every overflow hole is clear.

A new self-watering container needs a few weeks before you rely on it. Water young plants from the top until their roots have grown down into the wicking zone. Because water in these pots rarely flushes through, fertilizer salts can collect in the top layer of mix; water heavily from above every few weeks so the excess leaves through the overflow.

FAQ

Can I convert a normal pot into a self-watering one?

Yes. Stand the pot in a deep tray with a layer of gravel or an upturned saucer as a platform, and add a wick through a drainage hole into the water below. The result is less tidy than a purpose-made container, but the principle is the same.

Do self-watering pots cause root rot?

Not if the overflow hole works and the mix above the platform is a free-draining container mix. Problems start when the overflow is blocked or the pot stands in a saucer that keeps the water level above the platform.

Are self-watering containers good for succulents?

Usually not. Plants that need the mix to dry out between waterings, such as most succulents and many Mediterranean herbs, prefer a normal pot. A deep watering before you go usually carries them through.

How big a reservoir do I need for two weeks?

Measure your plant's daily use in a test run, multiply by the number of days, and add a margin for hot weather. The holiday planner gives an estimate if you have not tested yet.
