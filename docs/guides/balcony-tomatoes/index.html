Tomatoes on a Balcony: Container Size and Sun

Tomatoes are worth growing on a balcony if one spot gets at least 6 hours of direct sun in summer, and that spot is usually right at the railing, not against the back wall. Match the pot to the plant type: a compact dwarf or bush tomato is happy in a few gallons, while a tall vining tomato needs a much larger container, firm support and steady watering.

Know which type of tomato you have

Garden tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum) come in two growth habits, and the label or seed packet usually says which.

  • Determinate (bush) tomatoes grow to a set height, set most of their fruit over a few weeks and then slow down. They need only light support. Dwarf and "patio" types are compact determinates, some small enough for a railing box or hanging basket.
  • Indeterminate (cordon or vining) tomatoes keep growing and fruiting until frost or cold stops them. Grown as a single stem (a cordon) they can easily reach 6 ft (1.8 m) or more, so on a balcony the ceiling above can become the limit. Pinch off side shoots to keep one main stem.

Cherry tomatoes of either habit are the most forgiving choice for a balcony: they ripen faster, crack less and still produce in slightly less sun than large slicing tomatoes.

Container size by plant type

A tomato in too small a pot dries out within hours on a hot day, and that is the root of most balcony tomato problems. Bigger pots hold more water and keep the temperature of the root zone steadier. The figures below are practical working sizes in line with common extension and almanac guidance; treat the lower number as a minimum.

Weight is the other side of a large pot. A 10 gallon (38 L) container of wet potting mix often weighs 60 lb (27 kg) or more before you add the pot and plant. Use the Pot Weight Planner to add up your containers, place heavy pots near the building wall or along load-bearing edges your building manager names, and read how much weight a balcony can hold before buying several large tubs. A lighter potting mix helps, but it also dries faster.

Sun hours: why the railing wins

Tomatoes need about 6 to 8 hours of direct sun for a good crop. With less, plants grow tall and leafy, set few flowers and ripen slowly.

On a balcony with another balcony above it, the ceiling decides a lot. When the summer sun is high around midday, the ceiling edge throws a shadow across the back of the floor. A point on the balcony gets sun only if the line from it to the sun passes under the ceiling edge. In simple terms, for a balcony facing the midday sun:

sunlit distance from the railing ≈ (ceiling height − height of the leaves) ÷ tan(sun elevation)

At about 45°N, the noon sun stands roughly 68° above the horizon at the June solstice (tan 68° ≈ 2.5). With a ceiling 8 ft (2.4 m) above the floor, leaves at 2 ft (0.6 m) above the floor are in noon sun only up to about (8 − 2) ÷ 2.5 = 2.4 ft (0.7 m) in from the railing. Leaves at 5 ft (1.5 m) get noon sun only within about 1.2 ft (0.4 m) of the railing. Tall cordon tomatoes should therefore stand right at the front.

Worked example: finding the 6-hour spot

The formula covers only noon. To find a spot with 6 hours or more, check the whole day. Take an example balcony in Minneapolis (about 45° N) facing south-east, 5 ft (1.5 m) deep and 10 ft (3 m) wide, with side walls, the slab above at 8 ft (2.4 m) and an open railing 3.5 ft (1.07 m) high. The Sun & Shade Simulator gives these clear-sky hours for July 15 (times in local solar time; on the clock in Minneapolis add about 1 h 20 min for daylight saving time and the position in the time zone):

  • Railing (at railing height, e.g. a box hung on the inside of the railing): sun from about 4:50 a.m. to 1:05 p.m., 8.3 hours. A good spot for a cordon tomato in a pot pushed against the railing.
  • Middle (a pot 2.5 ft in, foliage at about 1 ft): sun from about 5:50 a.m. to 11:50 a.m., 6.0 hours. Just enough for a bush or dwarf tomato; taller foliage gets less, because the slab above cuts in sooner.
  • Back wall (1 ft from the wall): sun from about 7:00 to 10:40 a.m., 3.6 hours. Not a tomato spot.

Checking by hand works too: mark the three spots with tape and note at every full hour on a clear day whether direct sun touches the leaves.

On this balcony one indeterminate tomato in a 10 gallon pot goes in the railing corner that gets sun longest, a dwarf tomato goes in a railing box next to it, and the middle and back are left for part-shade herbs. The simulator shows the same three spots for every month, so you can see how long the railing spot stays above 6 hours into September. In the Southern Hemisphere, the sunny side is north-facing instead of south-facing.

Support without drilling

Many leases forbid drilling into the facade, ceiling or balcony slab. You do not need to.

  • Stake inside the pot: push a sturdy stake to the bottom of the container at planting time and tie the stem loosely with soft ties every 8 to 12 in (20 to 30 cm).
  • Tomato cage: fine for bush types; choose a heavy one, since light wire cages fold over with a laden plant.
  • Trough with built-in trellis: the weight of the wet mix anchors the frame.
  • Strap to the railing: if your building allows it, tie the stake to the railing with removable straps so the plant cannot blow over. Nothing should lean over the outer edge.
A tall tomato in full leaf catches a lot of wind. On upper floors, secure the pot or the stake so the plant cannot tip, and never stand pots on top of the railing. The Wind Exposure Check tells you how exposed your spot is.

Watering and blossom end rot

Blossom end rot is a dark, leathery patch on the bottom of the fruit. It is caused by a shortage of calcium in the developing fruit, but in containers the reason is usually not a lack of calcium in the mix. Calcium travels with water, and when the pot dries out between waterings the fruit misses its supply. Even watering is the cure; adding calcium seldom helps if watering stays irregular.

In practice: water deeply until it drains, check large pots every day in summer and small pots morning and evening in heat waves, and use a saucer or a self-watering container to bridge hot afternoons. The Watering Rhythm & Holiday Planner shows which of your pots dries fastest. Wind dries pots faster too. In a heat wave, flowers often drop without setting fruit; this usually stops when nights cool down.

FAQ

Can I grow tomatoes on a north-facing balcony?

Rarely with success. North-facing balconies usually get far less than 6 hours of direct sun. A dwarf cherry tomato at the railing may give a small crop if the balcony is open to the sides.

What is the smallest pot for a tall tomato?

About 5 gallons (19 L) is a bare minimum; 10 gallons (38 L) or more is far easier to keep evenly moist.

Do I need to hand-pollinate tomatoes on a high floor?

Tomato flowers pollinate themselves when they are shaken. Wind usually does this; on a very sheltered balcony, tap the flowering stems gently every day or two.
