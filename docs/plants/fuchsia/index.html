Fuchsias on a balcony

Fuchsias are the classic flowers for the shady side of the building. Hybrid fuchsias flower from early summer to fall in baskets and pots where petunias and geraniums would sulk – on north- and east-facing balconies and under a deep slab. Their limits are heat and hot afternoon sun, not shade. In most of the US they are kept over winter in a cool, bright place or grown new each year.

Light: shade is the point

The Missouri Botanical Garden lists hybrid fuchsias for part to full shade, and Clemson HGIC says they need protection from hot afternoon sun. On a balcony that means bright light without direct midday or afternoon sun: a north-facing balcony, the back half of an east-facing one, or a west-facing balcony where the slab above keeps the plants in shade until late afternoon.

The Sun & Shade Simulator helps find that spot. Look for 0 to 4 hours of direct sun in June, ideally in the morning; on a north-facing balcony the railing may get a little early and late sun in summer, which fuchsias handle well. A spot with 6 hours or more of sun is better left to sun plants. More plants for this side are in north-facing balcony plants.

Pot, basket and weight

Trailing fuchsias are made for hanging baskets and the front of railing boxes; upright types and standards (fuchsias trained as a small tree) go in pots. Use a 12 to 14 in (30 to 35 cm) basket or pot for one to three plants and a quality potting mix that holds moisture but drains freely.

The Pot Weight Planner gives about 12.5 L of mix and 21 lb (9.4 kg) wet weight for a 12 in round pot in standard mix. A bowl-shaped 14 in basket holds less mix than a pot of the same width – roughly 10 to 15 L – but soaked, with liner and plants, still weighs around 20 to 30 lb (9 to 14 kg) on a single ceiling hook. Make sure the hook is rated for that and fixed only where your building allows.

Heat and wind

Heat is the fuchsia's real limit. Clemson HGIC notes that flowering stops when the average daily temperature rises above 76 °F (24 °C). In the Deep South and the hot interior, standard hybrids struggle in midsummer; Clemson suggests cultivars of F. magellanica and the Triphylla Group, such as 'Gartenmeister Bonstedt', for heat tolerance – MBG notes 'Gartenmeister' tolerates more sun than other hybrid fuchsias. In cooler coastal and northern climates, fuchsias flower all summer.

Fuchsia stems are brittle, and baskets swing. On a balcony the Wind Exposure Check rates Exposed or Very exposed, hang baskets low and close to the wall, or grow upright fuchsias in heavy pots at floor level. A solid or glass railing gives useful shelter at pot height.

Watering

Keep the mix evenly moist through the season. Clemson HGIC advises watering when the surface feels dry, and baskets and boxes may need water daily or even twice a day in hot weather. A fuchsia that dries out drops its buds and flowers. On the other hand, a basket standing wet in cool weather rots; check before you water.

Shade slows drying: in the Watering & Holiday Planner, a 12 in plastic pot with a large fuchsia in 3 hours of sun on warm days comes out at "check every 2–3 days", while the same plant in a basket in heat needs daily checks. Baskets dry from the top and the sides; water slowly until it runs out at the bottom.

Feeding and pinching

Fuchsias flower on new growth and need regular feeding: a liquid fertilizer for flowering plants every one to two weeks from late spring to late summer, at the label rate. Pinch the tips of young plants two or three times in spring to make them bushy. Remove spent flowers with their seed pods, or the plant puts energy into berries instead of new flowers.

Overwintering by USDA zone

MBG gives hybrid fuchsias as hardy in zones 10 and 11. The hardy fuchsia, F. magellanica, and some of its hybrids survive considerably colder winters in the ground, often regrowing from the base, but in a pot their roots are more exposed. Check your zone on the USDA map.

  • Zones 10–11: fuchsias can stay out; shelter them from the occasional cold night.
  • Colder zones: bring them in before the first frost. Clemson HGIC recommends reducing water gradually in fall, cutting very large plants back to the rim of the pot, and overwintering in a cool place around 45 to 50 °F (7 to 10 °C). Water sparingly – just enough that the mix does not dry out completely. Illinois Extension lists fuchsia among the plants that can survive in an unheated but frost-free garage.
  • Cuttings: fall cuttings, kept in bright, indirect light in a cool room, are a space-saving alternative.

In early spring, bring overwintered plants into more light and warmth, trim them, repot into fresh mix and start feeding. Do not put them out until frosts are over. More methods in overwintering balcony plants.

Common mistakes

  • A south or west railing in full sun. Leaves scorch and flowers stop.
  • Letting a basket dry out. Buds drop within a day.
  • No feeding. Flowering fades by midsummer.
  • Overwintering in a warm room. Plants grow weak and get whiteflies; a cool, bright place is better.
  • Baskets on the windward side. Brittle stems snap.

FAQ

Why does my fuchsia drop its buds?

Most often the mix dried out once, or the plant got too hot. Moving it to a shadier, cooler spot usually helps.

Can fuchsias grow in full shade?

Yes, in bright shade they flower well; in deep, dark shade under a roof they flower less.

Are fuchsias toxic to pets?

The ASPCA lists fuchsia as non-toxic to cats and dogs.
