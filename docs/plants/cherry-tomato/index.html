Cherry tomatoes on a balcony

Cherry tomatoes are the most rewarding tomatoes for a balcony. They ripen sooner than large slicing tomatoes, split less and still crop where the railing gets six hours of sun. The decisions that matter are the plant type – dwarf, bush or tall – the size of the pot and a watering routine that never lets the pot dry out. This profile covers cherry types specifically; the general tomatoes on a balcony guide explains the ceiling-shadow geometry in detail.

Dwarf, bush or tall?

University of Minnesota Extension describes the two growth habits: determinate (bush) tomatoes stop at a set size and ripen their fruit over a shorter period, while indeterminate (vining) types keep growing and producing until cold weather and need sturdier support. For cherry tomatoes on a balcony that translates into three practical groups:

  • Dwarf and micro cherries – 1 to 2 ft (30 to 60 cm) tall, for rail boxes, small pots and hanging baskets. Lower yields, no staking.
  • Bush cherries – 2 to 4 ft (60 to 120 cm), in a 5 gal pot with a short stake or cage.
  • Tall cherries – grown on a single stem, they reach 6 ft (1.8 m) or more and give the largest harvest over the longest season, but need a big pot, a tall stake and a spot right at the railing.

Light: how many sun hours?

Tomatoes need 6 to 8 hours of direct sun. On a balcony with a slab above, the leaves of a tall plant near the back wall are often in shade at midday, while a plant at the railing gets several more hours. In the Sun & Shade Simulator, look for a railing value of at least 6 hours in July and August, when the fruit ripens. South-east to south-west facing balconies are ideal; on an east- or west-facing balcony a cherry type is the safer choice than a beefsteak.

Pot size and weight

Most balcony tomato problems start with a pot that is too small, because a small pot dries out within hours. Use at least 2 to 3 gal (8 to 11 L) for a dwarf, 5 gal (19 L) for a bush type and 10 gal (38 L) or more for a tall cherry. Fill with a quality potting mix, not garden soil.

Large pots are heavy. The Pot Weight Planner gives about 49 L (12.9 gal) of mix for an 18 in (45 cm) round pot 16 in deep – about 81 lb (37 kg) wet in standard potting mix, plus the pot and a plant that may weigh several pounds in fruit. Two tall tomatoes in such pots are already 170 lb or more on one small area. Place them near a load-bearing edge your building manager names and read balcony weight limits first.

Wind and heat

A tall tomato in full leaf acts like a sail. On balconies the Wind Exposure Check rates Exposed or Very exposed, grow bush or dwarf cherries, or tie a tall plant to a stake every 8 to 12 in (20 to 30 cm) and secure the stake so the pot cannot tip. Never stand pots on the railing. Wind also dries pots: in the Watering Planner, raising the wind factor for an 18 in pot in hot weather shortens the interval noticeably.

Very hot weather is a limit of its own. Tomatoes drop flowers without setting fruit when days are very hot and, especially, when nights stay warm; fruit set usually resumes when nights cool. Afternoon shade does not cure it, but keeping the pot well watered helps the plant through the spell.

Watering

Water deeply and evenly. UMN notes that irregular moisture during fruiting leads to blossom-end rot – a dark, leathery patch at the bottom of the fruit caused by a calcium shortage in the developing fruit, which in pots almost always follows dry spells. Uneven watering also makes cherry tomatoes split after a big drink.

In summer check large pots every day and water until it drains. The Watering & Holiday Planner estimates more than 2 L a day for a large plant in an 18 in pot in 8 hours of sun on hot days and suggests checking every 1 to 2 days; for a week away it calculates a reservoir of over 13 L. A self-watering container or a drip line with a reservoir makes tomatoes much easier on a balcony.

Feeding and pruning

Mix slow-release fertilizer into the potting mix at planting, then feed with a liquid tomato fertilizer every one to two weeks once the first fruits set. UMN warns that too much nitrogen gives leafy plants that are slow to fruit. On tall cherries, pinch out the side shoots that grow between the leaf and the main stem to keep a single stem; bush and dwarf types are not pruned.

End of season

Tomatoes are frost-tender annuals in every USDA zone of the mainland US. UMN advises picking all fruit before the first hard frost; mature green fruit can ripen indoors. Then empty the pot – old tomato mix carries diseases – and start fresh next year. In very mild zones, a plant may survive a while longer, but it rarely produces well in its second season.

Common mistakes

  • Too small a pot. The plant wilts every afternoon and fruit gets blossom-end rot.
  • A tall type at the back wall. The slab above shades the leaves at midday.
  • Too much nitrogen. Big, dark plants with few flowers.
  • No support in wind. Stems snap and pots tip.
  • Reusing the same mix. Diseases carry over; use fresh mix each year.

FAQ

Which cherry tomato is best for a railing box?

A dwarf or "micro" variety labelled for containers or baskets. Taller types need more root room than a railing box offers.

Do balcony tomatoes need hand pollination?

Tomato flowers pollinate themselves when shaken. On a very sheltered balcony, tap the flower clusters every day or two.

Why are my cherry tomatoes splitting?

Usually a dry spell followed by heavy watering or rain. Even watering reduces it.

Are tomato plants toxic to pets?

The ASPCA lists the tomato plant as toxic to dogs, cats and horses: the leaves and stems contain solanine. Ripe fruit is listed as non-toxic. Keep the plant out of reach of pets that chew leaves.
