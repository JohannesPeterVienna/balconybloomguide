Balcony crop and plant reference

Which crop needs which pot, how much sun, and when can it go out? This page puts the figures two university extension services publish into one table, adds what the smallest suitable container weighs when wet, and checks overwintered plants against your USDA zone. Every column names its source; where a source is silent, the cell says so.

How to read it. The frost class says how the crop relates to the last spring frost; the Frost Date & Sowing Planner turns the rule into calendar dates for your city. The light column carries the two figures University of Maryland Extension gives for container vegetables: 6–8 hours of direct sun for warm-season crops, naming tomato, pepper, eggplant and squash, and 3–5 hours for cool-season crops, naming lettuce, spinach and Asian greens. Only the crops Maryland names have a figure in the table; for every other row the cell says "not given" rather than assuming that, say, potatoes or broccoli manage on the hours of a lettuce. Enter the hours the Sun & Shade Simulator gives for your spot and the table keeps the rows whose figure starts at or below them; rows without a figure are hidden while that filter is set.

Herbs and strawberries have a container size in the Maryland list but no frost class in the Colorado guide, so those cells stay empty rather than being filled from memory. The ten plant profiles cover basil, rosemary, lavender and strawberries in detail.

Maryland lists three sizes outside the groups: potatoes need 30 gallons of potting mix, dwarf fruit trees 25–30 gallons and shrubs 25 gallons; strawberries need a depth of 8 inches. Thirty gallons of wet standard mix weigh about 188 lb (85 kg) before the container – a load to clear with the building first.

Cross-check. The same Maryland pages state that a 20-inch container filled with moist growing medium and plants "can weigh 100 lbs". The Pot Weight Planner gives 121 lb (55 kg) for a round plastic pot 20 in across the top, 16 in at the base and 18 in tall in standard mix, without the plant (70 L of mix). The two figures agree in size; the planner's is higher because this pot is deep, and it shows why the weight has to be worked out per pot rather than guessed.

The rule behind the table: the USDA zone of a plant describes it growing in the ground. Penn State Extension points out that the roots of a container plant above ground can be as cold as the winter air and advises choosing plants rated two zones colder than your own, in larger pots and with some protection. No row here is less cautious than that: a pot is listed as staying outside only where the zone is at least two above the plant's coldest zone. Where the sources of a profile are more cautious still, the table follows them – English lavender is rated to zone 5, yet a pot stays out unprotected only from zone 8, because wet, cold roots kill it; a bigleaf hydrangea is never left without shelter, because its flower buds are less hardy than the plant. The middle column is the band in which the profile describes shelter against the wall and a wrapped pot; see overwintering balcony plants.

These zone bands are stored once, in the site's data file. The profiles of lavender and rosemary, the overwintering guide, the plant overview and this table all print them from there, and the build fails if a page is missing them.

The ground zones were taken from this site's plant profiles, where each is traced to its source (mostly the Missouri Botanical Garden Plant Finder). They were checked when the profiles were published on November 4, 2026; the Plant Finder could not be reached again from our build environment on November 18, so that check was not repeated.

How this page was compiled

The crop figures were copied by hand from the two extension publications on November 18, 2026 into one data file, which both this page and the frost planner read; an automated test checks that every crop row has a valid class and container group and that the weights on the page equal volume times wet weight. Nothing in the tables comes from our own growing trials – we have none. Where the sources give a range, the table keeps the range.

What is missing: variety differences (a dwarf tomato and a tall one share a row), flowers, and crops that neither source lists. Days to harvest are Colorado figures for open ground. If you find an error, tell us which row and which source.
