Rosemary on a balcony

Rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus, formerly Rosmarinus officinalis) is an evergreen Mediterranean shrub that grows well in a pot on a sunny balcony and gives fresh sprigs all year. It likes sun, sharp drainage and moderate water – and, unlike lavender, it does not forgive being dried out completely. In most of the United States it needs shelter in winter.

Light: how many sun hours?

Rosemary needs full sun. The Missouri Botanical Garden lists it for full sun and dry to medium moisture. On a balcony, plan for at least 6 hours of direct sun in summer; 8 or more gives denser, more aromatic growth. In less light it grows long and thin, and the mix stays wet longer, which invites root rot and powdery mildew.

Use the Sun & Shade Simulator to find the spot. On a south- or west-facing balcony the railing and often the middle reach 6 hours; on an east-facing one the railing may be the only place. In the cooler months the sun is lower and reaches deeper under the slab, so a winter spot near the back wall can get more sun than it does in June. Herbs that manage with less sun are covered in herbs for part shade.

Pot size and weight

Start a nursery plant in a pot about 12 in (30 cm) across and move it up one size every two to three years, in spring. A mature upright rosemary of 3 ft (90 cm) needs 14 to 16 in. Unglazed terracotta suits it; plastic keeps the mix wetter, which can be an advantage in hot, windy places if you water carefully.

Use a free-draining potting mix with grit or perlite added. The Pot Weight Planner gives about 12.5 L (3.3 gal) of mix for a 12 in pot – about 21 lb (9.4 kg) wet in standard mix – and about 32 L (8.6 gal), or 54 lb (24 kg), for a 16 in pot. Add 10 to 20 lb for a terracotta pot of that size. A large rosemary in terracotta is one of the heavier items on a small balcony; place it near the building wall.

Wind and heat

The needle-like leaves lose little water and do not tear, so rosemary copes well with wind and with reflected heat from walls and glass railings. In the classes Exposed and Very exposed of the Wind Exposure Check, the risk is the pot, not the plant: a tall rosemary in a light plastic pot catches the wind and tips. Use a wide, heavy pot, or choose a lower, spreading form (Prostratus Group), which also looks good trailing over the edge of a large container.

Watering

Rosemary is drought-tolerant in the ground but not in a small pot. Water thoroughly when the top inch or two (3 to 5 cm) of mix is dry, and empty the saucer afterwards. In summer that is often every two to three days; in a heat wave, more. The Watering & Holiday Planner estimates "check every 2–3 days" for a 12 in terracotta pot in 8 hours of sun on warm days.

The balance is narrow. A rosemary left standing in water rots; one allowed to dry out completely often turns brown and does not recover, even though the leaves look unchanged for a few days. Check the weight of the pot: a light pot needs water.

Feeding, pruning and harvest

Feed lightly – a slow-release fertilizer in spring or a half-strength liquid fertilizer once a month in the growing season. Too much nitrogen gives soft growth with less flavor.

Harvest by cutting shoot tips of 2 to 4 in (5 to 10 cm), always above a pair of leaves, and never take more than about a third of the plant at once. Shape the plant after flowering in late spring. Like lavender, rosemary regrows poorly from old, leafless wood.

Overwintering by USDA zone

MBG gives rosemary as hardy in zones 8 to 10. Some cultivars are tougher: Clemson HGIC names 'Arp' and 'Hill Hardy' as reliably hardy types in upstate South Carolina. A pot is colder than the ground, so Penn State Extension's rule – plan two zones colder for containers – applies, and the zones below are given for rosemary in a pot. Check your zone on the USDA map.

  • Zone 9 and warmer: leave it out all winter in a sheltered spot; cover it or move it against the house wall on the rare hard frost.
  • Zone 8: leave it out only in a sheltered spot against the house wall, out of rain, with the pot wrapped; bring it in whenever a hard frost is forecast.
  • Zone 7 and colder: overwinter it indoors and bring it in before the first hard frost. The best place is bright and cool, about 40 to 55 °F (4 to 13 °C): an unheated sunroom, a cool stairwell window or a frost-free garage with a window. Water sparingly, but do not let it dry out.

In a warm, dry living room rosemary often suffers from powdery mildew and spider mites; the cooler the bright spot, the better. See overwintering balcony plants.

Common mistakes

  • Letting the pot dry out completely. The plant may not show it for days, then turns brown.
  • Wet feet. A saucer of water or a dense mix rots the roots, especially in winter.
  • Too little light indoors. A dark corner over winter weakens it; it needs the brightest cool window.
  • Overharvesting a young plant. Leave at least two thirds of the foliage.
  • Light pots in wind. Upright rosemary tips over in gusts; weight the pot.

FAQ

Can I grow rosemary with lavender in one pot?

They like similar conditions, but rosemary needs a little more water. Separate pots make watering easier.

Why is my rosemary turning brown from the tips?

Most often the root ball dried out once, or the roots rotted in wet mix. Check the mix and the drainage hole before anything else.

Is rosemary safe around pets?

The ASPCA lists rosemary as non-toxic to cats and dogs.
