Ornamental grasses on a balcony

Ornamental grasses are the answer for the windy balcony. Their narrow leaves bend instead of tearing, they move beautifully in a breeze and many stay attractive well into winter. The group is broad, though: some grasses are hardy perennials that need cold winters, some are tender and grown as annuals, and a few like shade. Choosing the right grass for your sun hours and your USDA zone matters more than any care routine.

University of Minnesota Extension notes that it knows of no tall grasses of 4 ft or more for shade; for shady balconies it suggests shorter plants such as Hakone grass and sedges.

Light: how many sun hours?

Most ornamental grasses want full sun – 6 hours or more of direct sun in summer. In less light they flop and flower poorly. Hakone grass and many sedges are the exceptions and do best with morning sun or bright shade. Read the railing, middle and back-wall values in the Sun & Shade Simulator: a sunny railing suits fescue or fountain grass, a shady back corner Hakone grass.

Pot size and weight

Give grasses room: crowded roots dry out quickly. Small tufted grasses grow well in 10 to 12 in (25 to 30 cm) pots, medium grasses in 14 to 16 in, and tall grasses used as a privacy screen need 18 in or long troughs. Use a free-draining potting mix; a little grit helps sun-loving grasses.

Tall grasses in wind need weight at the base. The Pot Weight Planner gives about 32 L of mix and 54 lb (24 kg) wet weight for a 16 in pot in standard mix, and about 49 L and 81 lb (37 kg) for 18 in. That weight keeps the pot from tipping – but it also adds up on the slab. Wide, low pots are more stable than tall, narrow ones.

Wind and heat

Grasses are the plants to choose when the Wind Exposure Check rates your balcony Exposed or Very exposed. Their leaves are narrow and flexible, they do not shred and they lose relatively little water in wind. What does suffer is the pot: wind dries it faster, and a tall grass in a light pot tips over. Group pots against the wall, use heavy-based containers and leave nothing loose on the railing. The guide to plants for windy balconies lists more wind-tolerant plants.

Sun-loving grasses tolerate heat well, including reflected heat from walls. Hakone grass scorches in hot afternoon sun.

Watering

In the ground, many grasses are drought-tolerant once established. In a pot they are not: water when the top inch or two of mix is dry, thoroughly, until it drains. On a windy, sunny balcony in summer that can mean every one to two days for a large grass. The Watering & Holiday Planner estimates around 1.4 L a day and "check every 1–2 days" for a 16 in plastic pot in 7 hours of sun on hot days with a Very exposed wind factor. Blue fescue prefers to be on the dry side; Hakone grass and sedges like steady moisture.

Feeding and cutting back

Feed lightly: a slow-release fertilizer in spring is enough for most grasses. Too much nitrogen makes them floppy. Cut back deciduous grasses such as switchgrass and fountain grass in late winter or early spring, before new growth starts, leaving a few inches of stubble; until then the dry stems look good and protect the crown. Evergreen blue fescue and sedges need only a comb-through to remove dead leaves. Divide clumps every few years when the center dies out, in spring.

Overwintering by USDA zone

The zone given on the label applies to grasses planted in the ground. Penn State Extension advises choosing container plants about two zones hardier than your zone, because roots in a pot above ground can get as cold as the air, and using large pots for insulation. Wisconsin Extension states that Hakone grass will not overwinter in an exposed container above ground: bury the pot in late fall or move it into a cool, sheltered spot such as an unheated garage. Clemson HGIC lists purple fountain grass as hardy only in zones 9 to 10. Check your zone on the USDA map.

  • Hardy grasses in pots (rated two zones colder than yours): leave out, group against the house wall, wrap the pots; water occasionally in dry spells when the mix is not frozen. Moist mix freezes more slowly than dry mix.
  • Grasses near their limit: move into an unheated garage or shed for the coldest months.
  • Tender grasses such as purple fountain grass: treat as annuals or overwinter in a bright, cool, frost-free place.

Frost-proof pots matter: unglazed terracotta can crack when wet mix freezes. See overwintering balcony plants.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing by looks, not zone. A tender fountain grass dies in a zone 6 winter.
  • Tall grass in a light pot. It tips in the first gust.
  • Cutting back in fall. Wait until late winter.
  • Shade grass in sun, sun grass in shade. Hakone grass scorches; fescue rots.
  • Forgetting winter water. Evergreen grasses in pots can dry out in sunny, windy winter weather.

FAQ

Which grass is best for a very windy, sunny balcony?

Low, tufted grasses such as blue fescue are the safest; taller grasses work in wide, heavy pots against the wall.

Can grasses be used as a privacy screen?

Tall grasses in long troughs make a soft screen in summer and fall; most are cut back in late winter, so the screen is gone until early summer.

Are grasses messy on a balcony?

Some shed seed heads and leaves in fall and winter. Sedges and blue fescue shed least.
