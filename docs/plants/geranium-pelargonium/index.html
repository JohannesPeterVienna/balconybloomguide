Geraniums (Pelargonium) on a balcony

The balcony "geranium" is a Pelargonium, not the hardy cranesbill Geranium of garden borders. Zonal geraniums (Pelargonium × hortorum) and trailing ivy-leaf geraniums (P. peltatum) flower from late spring to frost in a sunny railing box, cope with a day or two of dry mix and can be kept from one year to the next indoors. They are tender perennials: in most of the United States they are grown as annuals or brought in for winter.

Light: how many sun hours?

Zonal geraniums flower best in full sun, which for a balcony means at least 6 hours of direct sun on the leaves. The Missouri Botanical Garden recommends full sun with some afternoon shade in hot weather, and that matches balcony experience in the South and the interior: a west-facing railing box in Texas or Arizona can be too much in July, while the same box in Seattle or Boston is ideal.

Check the numbers at the railing, where the boxes hang. Open the Sun & Shade Simulator, enter your balcony and read the "railing" row for June and September. If the railing gets 6 hours or more, plant zonals. If it gets 4 to 5 hours, ivy-leaf geraniums usually still flower acceptably, and the middle of the balcony is better left to plants that like part sun. Below about 3 hours, geraniums stay leafy and stretch towards the light.

Pot size and weight

Geraniums do not need deep pots. They flower better when their roots fill the container, which is one reason they are the classic railing-box plant. A box 6 to 7 in (15 to 18 cm) deep is enough; spacing of about 8 to 10 in (20 to 25 cm) between plants gives each one enough mix to get through a hot afternoon.

Weight is what matters on the railing. A 32 in (80 cm) plastic railing box about 8 in (20 cm) wide and 7 in (18 cm) deep holds around 20 L (5 gal) of mix. Wet standard potting mix weighs about 0.75 kg per litre in the Pot Weight Planner, so the box comes to roughly 35 to 40 lb (16 to 18 kg) with pot and plants – all of it hanging on the railing brackets. Read railing planters before you choose brackets, and keep the boxes on the inside of the railing.

Use a well-drained potting mix, never garden soil. MBG notes that poorly drained soil leads to stem and root rot, and that is the most common way balcony geraniums die.

Wind and heat

Geraniums are among the better choices for an exposed balcony. Their stems are stiff in zonals and flexible in ivy-leaf types, and the leaves are tough enough not to shred. On a balcony the Wind Exposure Check rates Exposed or Very exposed, choose compact zonals and ivy-leaf types over tall, top-heavy plants, pinch long shoots back and make sure the box is clamped, not just hooked over the rail.

Heat is less of a problem than wet. A geranium wilts less than a petunia on a hot afternoon and recovers when watered. Long spells of hot, humid, rainy weather are harder on them: MBG notes they can be difficult in summers like those of St. Louis, and leaf spots and gray mold appear when the plants stay wet.

Watering

Water when the top inch (2–3 cm) of mix is dry, then water thoroughly until it drains. Geraniums would rather be slightly too dry than constantly wet. Water the mix, not the leaves, preferably in the morning.

The Watering & Holiday Planner estimates the rhythm for your pot size, sun hours and wind. For a 12 in (30 cm) terracotta pot in 7 hours of sun on warm summer days it suggests checking every 2 to 3 days; the same pot in plastic in hot weather drops to every 1 to 2 days. Treat that as a starting point and check with your finger.

Feeding and grooming

Geraniums in pots use up the nutrients in the mix within a few weeks. Feed with a balanced liquid fertilizer for flowering plants every one to two weeks from late spring to late summer at the strength on the label, or work a slow-release fertilizer into the mix at planting. Too much nitrogen gives large leaves and few flowers.

Remove spent flower heads by snapping the whole flower stalk off at its base. This keeps new buds coming and stops dead petals rotting on the leaves.

Overwintering by USDA zone

MBG lists Pelargonium × hortorum as hardy in USDA zones 10 and 11. In those zones – coastal Southern California, South Florida, Hawaii – geraniums can stay on the balcony all year, with frost cloth on the rare cold night. Everywhere colder, bring them in before the first frost. Check your zone on the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map.

University of Minnesota Extension describes three ways to keep them:

  • As a potted plant: cut the plant back by about a third, pot it and keep it in a sunny window or under a grow light, where geraniums prefer daytime temperatures of 60 to 65 °F (16 to 18 °C) and slightly cooler nights. Water sparingly. Prune again in March to keep it compact.
  • As cuttings: take 3 to 4 in (8 to 10 cm) tip cuttings in late summer, root them in a well-drained medium and grow them on in bright, indirect light. They take up the least room and usually give the best plants next year.
  • Bare-root: shake off the soil and store the plants hanging or in a paper bag in a cool, dry place. This is the most demanding method and works poorly for scented-leaf types.

More options for tender plants are in the guide to overwintering balcony plants.

Common mistakes

  • Planting in the shady middle of the balcony. The slab above shades much of the floor in summer; the railing is the geranium spot.
  • Too much water. Constantly wet mix and saucers full of water cause stem and root rot. Empty saucers after rain.
  • No feeding. After June, unfed geraniums flower less and turn yellow-green.
  • Leaving them out for the first frost. One frosty night kills the top growth; bring them in when night temperatures approach freezing.

FAQ

Are balcony geraniums the same as hardy geraniums?

No. Balcony geraniums are Pelargoniums from southern Africa and are not frost-hardy. Hardy geraniums (cranesbills, genus Geranium) are perennials for beds and large containers.

Can geraniums grow on a north-facing balcony?

Only weakly. They need several hours of direct sun to flower; on most north-facing balconies, fuchsias, begonias or ferns do better.

Are geraniums toxic to pets?

The ASPCA lists geranium (Pelargonium) as toxic to cats and dogs; keep plants out of reach of pets that chew leaves.
