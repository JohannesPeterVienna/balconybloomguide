Strawberries on a balcony

Strawberries are the easiest fruit for a balcony: small plants, shallow roots and a crop within weeks of planting. The type you buy decides the shape of the harvest. On most balconies day-neutral varieties, which fruit from early summer to fall, give the most from a small box; June-bearers give one large crop. Sun at the railing and steady watering do the rest.

Which type to buy

University of Minnesota Extension distinguishes three types:

  • June-bearing plants produce one large, concentrated crop in early summer (mid-June to early July in Minnesota) and many runners.
  • Everbearing plants produce two crops, in early summer and early fall.
  • Day-neutral plants fruit through most of the growing season and make few runners. UMN names 'Seascape' and 'Albion' as good choices for containers.

For a balcony with room for one box or a few pots, day-neutral strawberries give a steady handful of berries from June to September. Alpine or woodland strawberries (Fragaria vesca) have small, intensely flavored fruit, make no runners and, according to UMN, grow well in part shade – the best choice for a balcony without 6 hours of sun.

Light: how many sun hours?

Garden strawberries need full sun to flower and ripen well: plan for at least 6 hours of direct sun, with 8 or more for the sweetest berries. Check the railing row of the Sun & Shade Simulator for June to September, since day-neutral types keep fruiting into fall when the sun is lower. On many east- and west-facing balconies, the railing reaches 6 hours from May to August; the back wall rarely does. A rail box on the inside of the railing is usually the best spot.

Pot size and weight

Strawberry roots are shallow, so depth matters less than volume per plant. A pot of about 8 in (20 cm) per plant, three plants in a 12 to 14 in pot, or plants 8 to 10 in (20 to 25 cm) apart in a rail box at least 6 in (15 cm) deep work well. Plant with the crown – the point where the leaves emerge – exactly at the surface: buried crowns rot, exposed ones dry out.

The Pot Weight Planner gives about 12.5 L of mix and 21 lb (9.4 kg) wet weight for a 12 in pot in standard mix. A 32 in rail box of around 20 L comes to roughly 35 to 40 lb (16 to 18 kg) with pot and plants. Hanging baskets and stacked "strawberry towers" save floor space but dry out fastest; the guide to strawberries in hanging baskets and rail boxes compares them.

Wind and heat

Strawberries grow low and cope with wind, but wind dries the shallow mix of a rail box quickly, and the flowers need pollinating insects. On an Exposed or Very exposed balcony in the Wind Exposure Check, fix boxes firmly on the inside of the railing and expect to water more often. On a very high, windy balcony with few bees, gently brushing the open flowers with a soft paintbrush helps fruit set; misshapen berries are often a sign of poor pollination.

In long spells of heat, plants flower and fruit less and berries get small. Afternoon shade from a neighbor's wall in July is no disadvantage in hot climates.

Watering

Keep the mix evenly moist. Dry spells while fruit is forming give small, seedy berries; constantly wet mix rots the crowns and invites gray mold. Water at the base of the plants, not over the fruit, preferably in the morning. In summer, rail boxes in full sun often need water every day.

The Watering & Holiday Planner estimates "check every 2–3 days" for a 12 in plastic pot in 7 hours of sun on warm days; a shallow rail box or basket, or a heat wave, shortens that to daily. Mulching the surface with straw keeps berries clean and the mix moist longer.

Feeding and runners

Day-neutral and everbearing plants keep growing and fruiting, so they need regular feeding. UMN suggests compost around the plants; in pots, a slow-release fertilizer at planting plus a liquid fertilizer for fruiting plants every two to three weeks from flowering onwards works well. Too much nitrogen gives big leaves and fewer berries.

Cut off runners unless you want new plants; they take energy from fruiting. If you do want them, pin a runner into a small pot of mix and cut it from the mother plant once it has rooted.

Overwintering by USDA zone

Garden strawberries are hardy perennials in the ground across most of the US, but the crowns and roots in a pot are exposed to the air temperature. Penn State Extension's rule for containers – plan for two zones colder – applies. UMN suggests that if you cannot insulate container strawberries, growing day-neutral types as annuals is simpler.

  • Zones 7 and warmer: leave pots out; group them against the house wall and mulch the crowns with straw.
  • Zones 5 and 6: group and wrap pots, cover the plants with straw after the first hard frosts, or move the pots into an unheated garage or shed.
  • Zones 4 and colder: overwinter pots in an unheated, frost-protected space, or replant in spring.

Plants are most productive in their first and second years; replace them after two to three seasons. More in overwintering balcony plants.

Common mistakes

  • Crown too deep. The plant rots; keep the crown at the surface.
  • Shallow boxes in full sun without daily water. Berries stay small and dry.
  • June-bearers for a small balcony. One short crop, then only leaves and runners.
  • Water over the fruit. Gray mold spreads on wet berries.
  • Keeping old plants too long. Yields fall after the second or third year.

FAQ

How many strawberries does one plant give?

It depends strongly on variety, sun and care; count on a few handfuls per plant over a season rather than bowls.

Can strawberries grow on a north-facing balcony?

Alpine strawberries can; large-fruited garden strawberries rarely ripen well with less than about 6 hours of sun.

Why are my berries misshapen?

Usually incomplete pollination, sometimes frost on the flowers or feeding by insects. Brushing the flowers helps on high balconies.

Are strawberry plants safe around pets?

The ASPCA lists strawberry as non-toxic to dogs, cats and horses.
